21. Matrix representations and change of basis

An operator acts on vectors; a matrix acts on their coordinates. A choice of bases connects the two descriptions. Keeping that distinction explicit turns change-of-basis formulas into a sequence of translations, and explains why similar matrices represent one operator in different coordinate languages.

Lessons 38–39 develop coordinate isomorphisms, matrix representations, composition, change of basis, similarity, and two proofs of row-rank equals column-rank. We repair the changing directions and indices in the captions by specifying each coordinate conversion before multiplying matrices.

21.1 Coordinates are a chosen isomorphism Lesson 38 · 01:11

Let $B=(v_1,\ldots,v_n)$ be a basis of $V$. If $v=\sum a_jv_j$, define its coordinate column $[v]_B=(a_1,\ldots,a_n)^{\mathsf T}$. Unique basis coefficients make this well defined. Its inverse takes a column $a$ to $\sum a_jv_j$. Coordinates of $au+bv$ are $a[u]_B+b[v]_B$, by distributing the two basis expansions and invoking uniqueness. Thus the coordinate map $c_B:V\to F^n$ is a linear bijection, or isomorphism.

The inverse of an isomorphism is linear by Chapter 20. Compositions of isomorphisms are linear and bijective: compose the inverse functions in reverse order to obtain an inverse. Consequently any two dimension-$n$ spaces over the same field are isomorphic via $c_C^{-1}c_B$. Conversely an isomorphism takes a basis to a basis, preserving spanning by surjectivity and independence by injectivity, so finite-dimensional isomorphic spaces have equal dimension.

Examples include $P_d(F)\cong F^{d+1}$ by coefficient columns, $M_{m,n}(F)\cong F^{mn}$ by a fixed ordering of entries, and the functions on a specified $n$-element set $\cong F^n$ by listing their values in a specified order. The ordering and basis choices belong to the construction. A bare vector space need not come with one distinguished coordinate system.

21.2 Columns are images of basis vectors Lesson 38 · 27:08

For a linear map $T:V\to W$, domain basis $B=(v_1,\ldots,v_n)$, and target basis $C=(w_1,\ldots,w_m)$, define $[T]^C_B$ to be the $m\times n$ matrix whose $j$th column is $[Tv_j]_C$.

Theorem 21.1, proved here. This is the unique matrix $A$ satisfying

$$[Tv]_C=A[v]_B\qquad(v\in V).$$

If $v=\sum a_jv_j$, linearity gives $[Tv]_C=\sum a_j[Tv_j]_C$. The column interpretation of multiplication says that this is $A(a_1,\ldots,a_n)^{\mathsf T}$. Conversely any matrix satisfying the identity must, on substituting $v=v_j$, have $j$th column $[Tv_j]_C$, because $[v_j]_B=e_j$. Hence all its entries are forced. ∎

The identity can also be written $c_CT=T_Ac_B$, a commutative square: applying the operator and then taking output coordinates agrees with taking input coordinates and then multiplying by the representing matrix. Solving for the map gives $T=c_C^{-1}T_Ac_B$. This expression proves that every matrix gives a unique map for the chosen bases.

Moreover $T\mapsto[T]^C_B$ is a linear isomorphism $\mathcal L(V,W)\to M_{m,n}(F)$. Its bijectivity follows from the preceding construction and uniqueness. Its linearity follows columnwise: $[(aS+bT)v_j]_C=a[Sv_j]_C+b[Tv_j]_C$. Therefore $\dim\mathcal L(V,W)=mn$. If either space is zero, the map space is zero and the corresponding empty-shape matrix space has its unique zero matrix, consistent with dimension zero.

For differentiation $D:P_3(\mathbb R)\to P_2(\mathbb R)$ in the monomial bases, the images $0,1,2X,3X^2$ give

$$[D]=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0&0\\0&0&2&0\\0&0&0&3\end{pmatrix}.$$

There are four input basis vectors and three output coordinates. The matrix is rectangular even though the derivative rule itself is usually written without any coordinates.

21.3 Composition forces the multiplication order Lesson 38 · 36:53

Proposition 21.2, proved here. For linear maps $T:U\to V$, $S:V\to W$ and bases $A,B,C$ of the three spaces,

$$[ST]^C_A=[S]^C_B[T]^B_A.$$

If $T:V\to V$ is invertible, then $[T^{-1}]^B_B=([T]^B_B)^{-1}$.

For every $u$, $[STu]_C=[S]^C_B[Tu]_B=[S]^C_B[T]^B_A[u]_A$. Uniqueness in Theorem 21.1 identifies the representing matrix. Apply this to $T^{-1}T$ and $TT^{-1}$: both representing products are the identity, proving the inverse formula. Conversely, if the representing square matrix $M$ is invertible, the map $c_B^{-1}T_{M^{-1}}c_B$ is a two-sided inverse of $T$, by direct composition. ∎

The middle coordinate basis must match. Multiplying representations of two maps while using incompatible bases for the intermediate space omits a conversion matrix and usually gives the wrong map. The rightmost matrix acts first, just as the rightmost function in a composition acts first.

21.4 The identity map changes coordinates Lesson 39 · 02:40

For two bases $B,C$ of one space, define $P_{C\leftarrow B}=[I]^C_B$. The arrow names its action:

$$[v]_C=P_{C\leftarrow B}[v]_B.$$

Its columns are the old basis vectors expressed in the new basis, $[v_j]_C$. The inverse is $P_{B\leftarrow C}$, since composition of the two coordinate translations is the identity in either direction. For a third basis $D$, $P_{D\leftarrow B}=P_{D\leftarrow C}P_{C\leftarrow B}$, again by applying both sides to every coordinate column.

In $\mathbb R^2$, let $E$ be standard and $B=((1,1),(1,-1))$. The matrix with these basis vectors as standard-coordinate columns is

$$Q=P_{E\leftarrow B}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad Q^{-1}=P_{B\leftarrow E}=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}.$$

Indeed $Q^2=2I$. Thus the vector $(3,1)$ has $B$ coordinates $(2,1)$, since $2(1,1)+(1,-1)=(3,1)$. This example requires characteristic different from two; over $\mathbb F_2$ its two proposed basis vectors coincide.

21.5 Change the input and output languages separately Lesson 39 · 09:04

Theorem 21.3, proved here. If $A=[T]^C_B$, then for new bases $B'$ of the domain and $C'$ of the target,

$$[T]^{C'}_{B'}=P_{C'\leftarrow C}\,A\,P_{B\leftarrow B'}.$$

Starting with new input coordinates, first convert to the old input coordinates, apply $A$, then convert the old output coordinates to the new ones:

$$[Tv]_{C'}=P_{C'\leftarrow C}[Tv]_C =P_{C'\leftarrow C}A[v]_B =P_{C'\leftarrow C}AP_{B\leftarrow B'}[v]_{B'}.$$

Uniqueness of the representation proves the formula. The right conversion runs from new input to old input, whereas the left conversion runs from old output to new output; remembering the path avoids guessing which inverse belongs where. ∎

For an operator use the same old basis on both sides and the same new basis on both sides. Set $Q=P_{B\leftarrow B'}$. Then

$$[T]_{B'}=Q^{-1}[T]_BQ.$$

For the lecture's real example $T(e_1)=e_1-e_2$, $T(e_2)=e_1+2e_2$, we have $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}$. In the basis $B=((1,1),(1,-1))$,

$$Q^{-1}AQ=\begin{pmatrix}3/2&-3/2\\1/2&3/2\end{pmatrix}.$$

Check its columns directly. The images of the new basis vectors are $(2,1)$ and $(0,-3)$. Solving $(a+b,a-b)=(2,1)$ gives $(a,b)=(3/2,1/2)$; solving it for $(0,-3)$ gives $(-3/2,3/2)$. Both methods give the same matrix.

21.6 Similarity is one operator in two bases Lesson 39 · 22:59

Square matrices $A,B$ over $F$ are similar if $B=Q^{-1}AQ$ for some invertible $Q$ over $F$.

The preceding theorem shows that two matrices of one operator are similar. Conversely, given this identity, take $T(x)=Ax$ on $F^n$ and choose the new basis consisting of the columns of $Q$. Those columns are independent because $Qc=0$ implies $c=Q^{-1}0=0$, and they span because $y=Q(Q^{-1}y)$. Their basis-to-standard conversion is exactly $Q$, so the new operator matrix is $B$.

Similarity is an equivalence relation: $A=I^{-1}AI$ proves reflexivity; rearranging $B=Q^{-1}AQ$ gives $A=QBQ^{-1}$ for symmetry; and $C=R^{-1}BR=(QR)^{-1}A(QR)$ proves transitivity. It is more restrictive than allowing unrelated input and output changes for a general map. The latter can simplify every rank-$r$ map to a rectangular identity block, as Problem 5 shows; an operator under similarity may resist diagonalization.

Studying the abstract operator separates its behavior from an arbitrary presentation. For example kernel dimension and range dimension remain unchanged by isomorphisms of coordinates: the coordinate maps give bijections from the abstract kernel to the matrix kernel and from the abstract range to the matrix range. Thus rank is independent of the bases. The final chapters ask how simple a matrix of one operator can become under this constrained change.

21.7 Two proofs that row rank equals column rank Lesson 39 · 37:52

For $A\in M_{m,n}(F)$, row rank is the dimension of the span of its rows in $F^n$; column rank is the dimension of the span of its columns in $F^m$. These are dimensions of different spaces, so their equality needs proof.

First proof: elimination and rank–nullity. Reversible row operations preserve the row span: each new row is a combination of old rows, and reversing the operations gives the other inclusion. In reduced row echelon form the $r$ nonzero rows are independent because their pivot columns form the identity pattern; a zero relation, inspected at each pivot column, has every coefficient zero. They span all the rows, so row rank is $r$.

Chapter 17's special null vectors form a basis of the kernel: assigning each free coordinate in turn to one and the other free coordinates to zero gives $n-r$ vectors, whose free-coordinate columns prove independence and whose arbitrary free assignments prove spanning. Rank–nullity gives column rank $n-(n-r)=r$. Thus the two ranks coincide. ∎

Second proof: a factorization and transpose. Suppose column rank is $r>0$. Extract $r$ original columns forming a column-space basis and assemble them into an $m\times r$ matrix $C$. Express each column of $A$ uniquely in this basis, and put its coefficients into an $r\times n$ matrix $R$. Then $A=CR$ by the column rule of multiplication. The row rule says every row of $A$ is a combination of the $r$ rows of $R$. Therefore row rank of $A$ is at most $r$, its column rank.

If $r=0$, every column is zero and both ranks are zero. Apply the proved inequality to $A^{\mathsf T}$: transposition exchanges row and column spans as coordinate lists, so column rank of $A$ is at most row rank of $A$. Combining inequalities proves equality over every field. ∎

Consequently the common rank is at most $\min(m,n)$. A matrix is full rank if equality holds. For a square matrix, full rank is equivalent to invertibility by Chapter 20. For a rectangular full-rank matrix, one of injectivity or surjectivity can still fail. Also, if $m<n$, rank–nullity guarantees a nonzero homogeneous solution: the kernel dimension is at least $n-m>0$.

21.8 Verify a coordinate change exactly

from sympy import Matrix

A = Matrix([[1, 1], [-1, 2]])
Q = Matrix([[1, 1], [1, -1]])
B = Q.inv() * A * Q
print(B.tolist())
v_new = Matrix([2, 1])
v_old = Q * v_new
assert Q * (B * v_new) == A * v_old
print(v_old.T.tolist()[0])
print((B * v_new).T.tolist()[0])
C = Matrix([[1, 0], [0, 1], [1, 1]])
R = Matrix([[1, 0, 2, 1], [0, 1, 3, -1]])
M = C * R
assert M.rank() == M.T.rank() == 2
print(M.rank(), len(M.nullspace()))
[[3/2, -3/2], [1/2, 3/2]]
[3, 1]
[3/2, 5/2]
2 2

All entries are exact rational numbers. The equality test translates the output of the new matrix back into old coordinates and compares it with applying the old matrix. The last example checks a rank-two column–row factorization with a two-dimensional kernel.

21.9 Problems with complete solutions

Problem 1. Reorder a basis

In a two-dimensional space let $C=(v_2,v_1)$ and $B=(v_1,v_2)$. Find the conversion matrix and the new matrix of $T$ if $[T]_B=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}$.

The coordinates swap, so $P_{C\leftarrow B}=P=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ and $P^{-1}=P$. Thus $[T]_C=P[T]_BP=\begin{pmatrix}d&c\\b&a\end{pmatrix}$. Equivalently the first new input is $v_2$, whose image $bv_1+dv_2$ has new coordinates $(d,b)$, verifying the first column.

Problem 2. Same rule, new polynomial coordinates

For real differentiation on $P_2(\mathbb R)$, find the operator matrix in $B=(1,X-a,(X-a)^2)$.

This is a basis: expansion by descending degree shows independence, and a length-three independent list in the dimension-three space spans. Its derivatives are $0,1,2(X-a)$, whose $B$ coordinates are $(0,0,0),(1,0,0),(0,2,0)$. Hence the matrix is $\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\0&0&2\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}$, the same numerical matrix as in the monomial basis. Equal matrices in two bases can occur; a change of basis does not always change the entries.

Problem 3. An invalid basis over another field

Why does $((1,1),(1,-1))$ fail to be a basis over a field of characteristic two, and why is it a basis otherwise?

In characteristic two, $-1=1$, so the two entries are identical and dependent. Otherwise a relation gives $a+b=0$ and $a-b=0$; subtraction gives $2b=0$, hence $b=0$ and then $a=0$. The independent length-two list is a basis of $F^2$. This justifies exactly when the factor $1/2$ in its conversion matrix is defined.

Problem 4. Identity is sensitive to two different bases

Must the matrix of the identity map be the identity matrix when different domain and target bases are used?

No. In $\mathbb R^2$, use $E=(e_1,e_2)$ for the domain and $C=(2e_1,e_2)$ for the target. The images $e_1,e_2$ have $C$ coordinates $(1/2,0),(0,1)$, so $[I]^C_E=\operatorname{diag}(1/2,1)$. With the same basis in domain and target the coordinates agree and the representing matrix is the identity. The underlying function in both cases fixes every vector.

Problem 5. Simplify a general map by two basis choices

Show that a rank-$r$ map $T:V\to W$ between finite-dimensional spaces has matrix $\begin{pmatrix}I_r&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$ for suitable independently chosen domain and target bases.

Use the adapted basis from rank–nullity, but order it as $u_1,\ldots,u_r,k_1,\ldots,k_s$, with the $k$ block a kernel basis. Then $Tu_1,\ldots,Tu_r$ is a basis of the range. Extend that image list to a basis of $W$. The first $r$ input basis vectors map to the corresponding first $r$ output basis vectors, and the remaining inputs map to zero, giving the displayed block matrix. If $V=W$, these two bases generally differ; this is not a proof that every operator is similar to that block matrix.

Problem 6. A quick obstruction to similarity

Show that the zero $2\times2$ matrix is not similar to $N=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$, although both square to zero.

Every conjugate $Q^{-1}0Q$ is zero, while $N$ has entry one in its upper-right position and is nonzero over every field. Equivalently similarity preserves rank, but these matrices have ranks zero and one. The shared equation $A^2=0$ is therefore too coarse to classify an operator; the least annihilating polynomial and kernel growth will provide finer information.
