14. Complex numbers and polynomial arithmetic

The real number $4$ solves $x^3=15x+4$, but a radical method reaches it through $(2+i)+(2-i)$. Enlarging the field makes an intermediate computation possible without changing the reality of its final answer. Formal polynomial arithmetic then tells us which parts of this story work over every field and which depend specifically on the complex numbers.

Lessons 25–26 develop complex arithmetic and formal polynomials through division and the fundamental theorem of algebra. We complete the algebraic checks and distinguish formal polynomials from their evaluation functions. Trigonometric functions and their addition formulas are assumed background. The fundamental theorem of algebra is stated without proof, as in the source, and its factorization consequences are proved.

14.1 Build the field on real pairs Lesson 25 · 21:25

Define $\mathbb C$ to be $\mathbb R^2$ with

$$(a,b)+(c,d)=(a+c,b+d),\qquad (a,b)(c,d)=(ac-bd,ad+bc).$$

Set $0=(0,0)$, $1=(1,0)$, $i=(0,1)$ and write $(a,b)$ as $a+bi$. This notation identifies a real $a$ with $(a,0)$, an injective map preserving its two operations and one. By the multiplication definition $i^2=-1$, and pair equality makes the real coordinates in $a+bi$ unique.

Addition is an abelian group coordinatewise, with inverse $(-a,-b)$. Multiplication is commutative by real commutativity, with identity $(1,0)$. To check its associativity, multiply $z=(a,b),w=(c,d),v=(e,f)$ in both orders. Each real coordinate is $ace-bde-adf-bcf$ and each imaginary coordinate is $acf-bdf+ade+bce$. Thus $(zw)v=z(wv)$. Distribution follows by expanding $z(w+v)$: its coordinates are $a(c+e)-b(d+f)$ and $a(d+f)+b(c+e)$, which split into the coordinate sums for $zw+zv$. Commutativity gives the other distributive law.

For $z=a+bi$, define its conjugate $\overline z=a-bi$. Direct multiplication gives $z\overline z=a^2+b^2$, positive when $z\ne0$. Consequently

$$z^{-1}=\frac{\overline z}{a^2+b^2}\quad(z\ne0),$$

because multiplying gives $1$. This verifies all field axioms, including inverses and $0\ne1$.

The real part and imaginary part are $\operatorname{Re}z=a$ and $\operatorname{Im}z=b$; both are real numbers. Expanding the coordinate formulas proves $\overline{z+w}=\overline z+\overline w$ and $\overline{zw}=\overline z\,\overline w$, while changing the sign twice gives $\overline{\overline z}=z$. Hence $z+\overline z=2\operatorname{Re}z$ and $z-\overline z=2i\operatorname{Im}z$.

14.2 Magnitude, a real scalar product, and triangle inequality Lesson 25 · 28:23

Define $|z|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\ge0$. The existence of nonnegative real square roots follows by the same bisection proof as Chapter 13: bracket a positive $t$ between squared endpoints $0$ and $B=\max(1,t)$, bisect preserving $a_n^2\le t\le b_n^2$, and use the error bound $|c^2-t|\le2B(b_n-a_n)$. The zero case is immediate and domain cancellation gives uniqueness of the nonnegative root.

We have $|z|^2=z\overline z$, $|z|=0$ exactly when $z=0$, and $|\overline z|=|z|$. Moreover $|zw|^2=zw\overline z\,\overline w=|z|^2|w|^2$, so nonnegative-root uniqueness gives $|zw|=|z||w|$.

For $w=u+vi$ and $z=x+yi$, define the real scalar product $\langle w,z\rangle_{\mathbb R}=ux+vy=\operatorname{Re}(w\overline z)$. This is the ordinary dot product of two real coordinate pairs. It is real valued; it is not the complex-valued inner product used in later courses.

Proposition 14.1, proved here. We have $|\langle w,z\rangle_{\mathbb R}|\le|w||z|$ and $|w+z|\le|w|+|z|$.

Expansion gives $(ux+vy)^2+(uy-vx)^2=(u^2+v^2)(x^2+y^2)$. Dropping the nonnegative second square and taking nonnegative square roots gives the scalar-product inequality. Next expand

$$|w+z|^2=|w|^2+2\langle w,z\rangle_{\mathbb R}+|z|^2 \le(|w|+|z|)^2.$$

Both sides being squares of nonnegative numbers, the claimed triangle inequality follows. ∎

In particular $|\operatorname{Re}z|,|\operatorname{Im}z|\le|z|$, by dropping one square in its definition. Applying triangle inequality to $w=(w-z)+z$ and then interchanging $w,z$ gives $\big||w|-|z|\big|\le|w-z|$. Induction gives $|z_1+\cdots+z_n|\le\sum|z_j|$, just as in the ordered real case.

14.3 Rotation, scaling, and choices of argument Lesson 25 · 37:23

For a nonzero complex number, ordinary plane trigonometry gives $z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)$ with $r=|z|>0$. An argument is any such angle $\theta$; changing it by $2\pi k$ for an integer $k$ gives the same point. Zero has no argument. A principal argument requires a chosen interval of length $2\pi$ and has a boundary convention.

Use $e^{it}:=\cos t+i\sin t$ as notation here. We are not deriving the exponential from a power series. The sine and cosine addition formulas give $e^{i(s+t)}=e^{is}e^{it}$, and the standard trigonometric values give $e^{i\pi}=-1$. Induction yields De Moivre's formula $(e^{i\theta})^n=e^{in\theta}$ for nonnegative integers $n$; inverse laws extend it to negative integers.

Thus $(re^{i\theta})(\rho e^{i\phi})=r\rho e^{i(\theta+\phi)}$: magnitudes multiply and arguments add modulo $2\pi$. Multiplication by a fixed nonzero $z$ rotates and scales every input. For $z\ne0$ and integer $n>0$, its $n$th roots are

$$r^{1/n}e^{i(\theta+2\pi k)/n},\qquad k=0,\ldots,n-1.$$

Positive real $n$th-root existence can be proved by real bisection with the same polynomial-difference bounds as for squares; it is taken as background in this trigonometric formula. Raising the listed numbers to the $n$th power verifies each, their arguments show they are distinct, and conversely any root must have magnitude $r^{1/n}$ and argument of the displayed form. The equation $w^n=0$ instead has only the root zero.

14.4 A real answer through nonreal intermediate values Lesson 25 · 07:26

A cubic $x^3+Ax^2+Bx+C=0$ is transformed by $x=y-A/3$ into

$$y^3+\left(B-\frac{A^2}{3}\right)y+ \left(\frac{2A^3}{27}-\frac{AB}{3}+C\right)=0.$$

This follows by expanding the substitution and cancelling its quadratic coefficient. Write the resulting equation as $y^3=3py+2q$. To find a solution set $y=u+v$ and impose the sufficient conditions $uv=p$ and $u^3+v^3=2q$, since then $(u+v)^3=u^3+v^3+3uv(u+v)$ is the desired equation.

For $p\ne0$, the numbers $u^3,v^3$ must solve the quadratic with sum $2q$ and product $p^3$. Its values are $q\pm\sqrt{q^2-p^3}$. Choose one cube root $u$ of the first nonzero value and set $v=p/u$. The product of the two values is $p^3$, so $v^3$ is the other one. This coordinated choice matters: independently choosing cube-root branches need not preserve $uv=p$. If $p=0$, solve $y^3=2q$ directly instead of dividing by a potentially zero $u$.

For $y^3=15y+4$, $p=5,q=2$, and $q^2-p^3=-121$. Direct multiplication gives $(2+i)^3=2+11i$ and $(2-i)^3=2-11i$, while $(2+i)(2-i)=5$. Thus $y=(2+i)+(2-i)=4$ satisfies the cubic. This is a precise example of a radical computation using nonreal intermediate values for a real result. No general impossibility theorem about every alternative real formula is required for that conclusion.

14.5 Two precise obstructions Lesson 25 · 41:57

No total order makes $\mathbb C$ an ordered field. In any ordered field a nonzero square is positive, while $1>0$ implies $-1<0$. Since $i\ne0$ but $i^2=-1$, these conclusions contradict each other. This excludes a compatible field order, not an arbitrary way of listing complex numbers. The order-completeness axiom of Chapter 13 consequently does not apply to $\mathbb C$.

There is also no real-bilinear associative unital multiplication on the usual real coordinate space $\mathbb R^3$ that extends $\mathbb C$ on its first two coordinates, with $1=(1,0,0)$ and $i=(0,1,0)$. Let $j=(0,0,1)$ and suppose $ij=a+bi+cj$ for real $a,b,c$, which is possible uniquely by coordinate equality. Associativity and $i^2=-1$ give $-j=i(ij)=ai-b+c(ij)=(ca-b)+(a+cb)i+c^2j$. Comparing third coordinates forces $-1=c^2$, impossible over $\mathbb R$. This argument uses the usual real vector operations and bilinearity; it makes no assertion about imposing unrelated field operations on an abstract set of the same cardinality.

14.6 A polynomial is a finite coefficient sequence Lesson 26 · 00:54

Let $R$ be a commutative unital ring. A formal polynomial is a function $a:\mathbb N_0\to R$ with only finitely many nonzero values. Its coefficient $a_k$ is the value at index $k$. Write $R[X]$ for all such polynomials. Equality means equality of every coefficient, independently of evaluation at field elements.

Add coefficients componentwise and multiply by convolution:

$$(ab)_n=\sum_{k=0}^n a_kb_{n-k}.$$

If $a_k=0$ beyond $r$ and $b_k=0$ beyond $s$, the sum is zero beyond $\max(r,s)$ and the product beyond $r+s$: for $n>r+s$, each term has either $k>r$ or $n-k>s$. Thus the operations remain within finite coefficient sequences.

The zero sequence and $(1,0,0,\ldots)$ are the two identities; additive inverses negate each coefficient. Additive group laws are coordinatewise. Multiplication is commutative by replacing $k$ with $n-k$ and using commutativity in $R$. For three polynomials, both parenthesizations have coefficient

$$\sum_{i+j+k=n}a_i b_j c_k,$$

because each ordered triple of nonnegative indices occurs once when expanding either convolution. Finite reassociation and reordering, proved in Chapter 8, justify that equality. Distribution follows by expanding $\sum a_k(b_{n-k}+c_{n-k})$ into the two corresponding finite sums. These checks make $R[X]$ a commutative unital ring.

Define $X$ itself to be the sequence with coefficient $1$ at index one and zero elsewhere. Induction on convolution shows $X^k$ has coefficient $1$ at index $k$ and zero elsewhere. Hence every sequence has the unique finite expression $a_0+a_1X+\cdots+a_rX^r$, recovering familiar notation without leaving the underlying object unspecified.

14.7 Degree and evaluation measure different information Lesson 26 · 20:53

For a nonzero polynomial, its degree is the largest index with a nonzero coefficient and its leading coefficient is that coefficient. A polynomial is monic if its leading coefficient is $1$. Set $\deg0=-\infty$, smaller than every integer; a nonzero constant has degree zero.

Proposition 14.2, proved here. If $R$ is an integral domain and $f,g\ne0$, then $\deg(fg)=\deg f+\deg g$. Hence $R[X]$ is an integral domain. Also $\deg(f+g)\le\max(\deg f,\deg g)$ with the zero convention.

Let $r=\deg f,s=\deg g$. All product coefficients beyond $r+s$ vanish as proved above. At $r+s$ the only potentially nonzero convolution term is $f_r g_s$: if one index is smaller than its maximum, the other is larger than its maximum. The product $f_rg_s$ is nonzero in a domain, proving the degree formula and the domain property. The sum bound follows since both sequences vanish beyond their largest degree. Cancellation of leading terms can make the sum's degree smaller, including the zero polynomial. ∎

For $t\in R$, evaluation sends $f$ to $f(t)=\sum a_k t^k$. Distribution and the power laws show that this map preserves addition, multiplication, and one. But evaluation functions need not distinguish formal polynomials. Over $\mathbb F_2$, $X$ and $X^2$ are different coefficient sequences but take the same value at both $0$ and $1$. The nonzero polynomial $X^2-X$ therefore defines the zero function on this field.

14.8 Long division is a terminating proof Lesson 26 · 36:29

Theorem 14.3, proved here. For a field $F$ and $f,g\in F[X]$ with $g\ne0$, there are unique $q,r\in F[X]$ such that $f=qg+r$ and either $r=0$ or $\deg r<\deg g$.

Start with $q=0,r=f$. If $r=0$ or has smaller degree than $g$, stop. Otherwise let $r$ have leading term $aX^n$ and $g$ leading term $bX^m$, with $n\ge m$ and $b\ne0$. Put $t=ab^{-1}X^{n-m}$ and replace $q$ by $q+t$ and $r$ by $r-tg$. The leading terms cancel, so the new remainder is zero or has strictly smaller degree. The identity $f=qg+r$ is invariant. Nonnegative degrees cannot strictly decrease forever, so the process terminates with the required remainder.

If $f=qg+r=q'g+r'$, then $(q-q')g=r'-r$. If $q\ne q'$, the left side is nonzero and has degree at least $\deg g$ by Proposition 14.2. The right side is zero or has degree below $\deg g$ by the sum bound, a contradiction. Thus $q=q'$ and then $r=r'$. ∎

The field hypothesis supplies $b^{-1}$. Division by a monic polynomial works over any commutative ring because $b=1$ needs no additional inverse. Arbitrary nonmonic division need not work over $\mathbb Z[X]$; dividing $X$ by the constant polynomial $2$ would require $X=2q$ with integer coefficients, which is impossible.

A Euclidean domain, in the division formulation used here, is an integral domain with a map $\delta$ from its nonzero elements to $\mathbb N_0$, such that division by nonzero $b$ gives $a=bq+r$ with either $r=0$ or $\delta(r)<\delta(b)$. The zero exception is required because $\delta(0)$ is not defined. Integers use absolute value; $F[X]$ uses degree. Some definitions also impose $\delta(a)\le\delta(ab)$ for nonzero factors; both examples satisfy it.

14.9 The Euclidean mechanism survives the change of objects

Let $f,g\in F[X]$ be not both zero. If one is zero, the other polynomial divided by its leading coefficient is their monic gcd. Otherwise repeated polynomial division terminates by decreasing nonnegative degrees. As with integers, $(f,g)$ and $(g,f-qg)$ have exactly the same common polynomial divisors. The last nonzero remainder, divided by its leading coefficient, is the unique monic greatest common divisor $d$. Here “greatest” means every common divisor divides $d$; numerical order on polynomials is unnecessary. Two such candidates divide each other, so their quotient is a nonzero constant by degree, and monicity forces that constant to be $1$.

Tracking combinations at each remainder step gives $d=uf+vg$ for polynomials $u,v$. Every common divisor divides this combination. Conversely, the terminal remainder divides the preceding remainders by reversing the division equations, hence divides $f,g$. This establishes the gcd's universal property and Bézout identity without merely asserting analogy.

A nonconstant polynomial is irreducible over $F$ if it cannot be written as a product of two positive-degree polynomials. If an irreducible $p$ does not divide $f$, its monic gcd with $f$ is $1$: a nonconstant divisor of $p$ would have to differ from $p$ by a nonzero constant. Bézout then gives $up+vf=1$. If $p\mid fg$, multiplying this equation by $g$ shows $p\mid g$. Thus irreducibles have the prime product property in $F[X]$.

Induction on degree now factors every nonconstant polynomial into a nonzero constant times monic irreducibles: either it is irreducible after normalizing, or split it into lower positive degrees and apply induction. Uniqueness follows from the prime product property: an irreducible in one factorization divides a factor in the other, which must be the same monic irreducible; cancel and induct. If one list ends, the remaining positive-degree product cannot equal a nonzero constant, by the degree formula. These are the precise ingredients of the integer factorization proof transported to polynomials.

14.10 Roots, linear factors, and the fundamental theorem Lesson 26 · 50:33

A root or zero of $f\in F[X]$ is a scalar $\lambda\in F$ such that $f(\lambda)=0$. Dividing by $X-\lambda$ gives $f=q(X-\lambda)+r$ with constant remainder $r$, because the divisor has degree one. Evaluation at $\lambda$ gives $r=f(\lambda)$. Thus $f(\lambda)=0$ exactly when $X-\lambda$ divides $f$; this is the factor theorem.

A nonzero degree-$n$ polynomial has at most $n$ distinct roots. Induct on $n$, with a nonzero constant having none. If a root $\lambda$ exists, remove the factor $X-\lambda$; every other root must be a root of the degree-$(n-1)$ quotient, since its value of $X-\lambda$ is nonzero and can be cancelled in the field. Induction gives the bound. Over an infinite field this also makes the passage from formal polynomials to evaluation functions injective: a nonzero difference cannot vanish at every scalar.

Fundamental theorem of algebra, stated without proof. Every nonconstant polynomial with coefficients in $\mathbb C$ has a root in $\mathbb C$.

Taking that theorem as an input, repeated application of the factor theorem proves that a degree-$n$ complex polynomial has a factorization $f(X)=a_n\prod_{j=1}^n(X-\lambda_j)$. Induct on degree: remove one root and factor the quotient; its leading coefficient remains $a_n$. Uniqueness up to permutation follows either from the irreducible factorization result or directly by evaluating two factorizations at the first root, matching a linear factor, cancelling in the formal polynomial domain, and inducting. Repeated roots are retained in the list; the number of distinct roots can be less than $n$.

For real coefficients, conjugation gives $f(\overline\lambda)=\overline{f(\lambda)}$, so nonreal roots occur with their conjugates, including multiplicity by uniqueness of the complex factorization. A paired factor is $(X-\lambda)(X-\overline\lambda)=X^2-2\operatorname{Re}(\lambda)X+|\lambda|^2$, real and without a real root when $\operatorname{Im}\lambda\ne0$, because it equals $(X-\operatorname{Re}\lambda)^2+(\operatorname{Im}\lambda)^2$. Thus real polynomials factor into real linear and irreducible quadratic factors. This difference between real and complex scalars will control later operator decompositions.

14.11 Exact computations with formal polynomials

import sympy as sp

X = sp.symbols("X")
quotient, remainder = sp.div(X ** 4 + 1, X ** 2 + 1, domain=sp.QQ)
assert sp.expand(quotient * (X ** 2 + 1) + remainder) == X ** 4 + 1
print(quotient, remainder)
print(sp.gcd(X ** 3 - 1, X ** 2 - 1))
print(sp.expand((2 + sp.I) ** 3))
print(sp.factor(X ** 3 - 15 * X - 4))
assert all((t * t - t) % 2 == 0 for t in range(2))
X**2 - 1 2
X - 1
2 + 11*I
(X - 4)*(X**2 + 4*X + 1)

The polynomial domain is explicitly rational, so division and gcd are exact. The imaginary unit is symbolic, and the final assertion distinguishes a nonzero formal polynomial from its zero evaluation function over $\mathbb F_2$.

14.12 Problems with complete solutions

Problem 1. All real roots of the cubic

After finding $4$ as a root of $X^3-15X-4$, find the other roots.

Division by $X-4$ gives $X^2+4X+1$, since $(X-4)(X^2+4X+1)=X^3-15X-4$. Completing the square gives $(X+2)^2=3$, so the other roots are $-2+\sqrt3$ and $-2-\sqrt3$. All three are real and distinct. The nonreal intermediates in the radical calculation did not imply that the resulting roots were nonreal.

Problem 2. A conjugate inverse

Compute $(3+4i)^{-1}$ and verify its magnitude.

The conjugate formula gives $(3-4i)/25$. Its product with $3+4i$ is $(9+16)/25=1$. The original magnitude is $5$, while the inverse magnitude is $\sqrt{(3/25)^2+(-4/25)^2}=5/25=1/5$, agreeing with $|z^{-1}|=|z|^{-1}$ from multiplicativity.

Problem 3. Four fourth roots

Find every complex solution of $z^4=-4$.

The magnitude must be $\sqrt2$, and the arguments are $\pi/4+k\pi/2$ for $k=0,1,2,3$. The roots are $1+i,-1+i,-1-i,1-i$. Each squares to either $2i$ or $-2i$, hence its fourth power is $-4$. They are four distinct roots of a degree-four nonzero polynomial, so the root bound excludes any others. Choosing a single principal fourth root would omit three valid answers.

Problem 4. A polynomial Bézout identity

Find the monic gcd of $X^3-1$ and $X^2-1$ over $\mathbb Q$ and give a certificate.

Subtracting $X(X^2-1)$ from $X^3-1$ gives $X-1$. Also $X^2-1=(X+1)(X-1)$, so the next remainder is zero and the monic gcd is $X-1$. The displayed subtraction is the Bézout identity $X-1=1\cdot(X^3-1)-X\cdot(X^2-1)$. It proves every common divisor divides $X-1$, while the factorizations show $X-1$ divides both inputs.

Problem 5. Formal equality is stronger than evaluation equality

Over $\mathbb F_2$, compare the polynomial $X^2-X$ with zero. Does its two-root behavior contradict the degree bound?

Its degree-two coefficient is $1\ne0$, so it is not the zero formal polynomial. At $0$ and $1$ it evaluates to zero, hence defines the zero function on the two-element field. It has exactly two roots, which equals its degree and does not contradict the bound. The inference from infinitely many roots to a zero polynomial works only when the scalar set really supplies infinitely many distinct points.

Problem 6. Where leading coefficients matter

Show why $\deg(fg)=\deg f+\deg g$ fails over a ring with zero divisors, using $\mathbb Z/6\mathbb Z$.

Take $f=2X$ and $g=3X$. Both formal polynomials have degree one because their leading coefficients are nonzero classes. But their product is $6X^2=0$, with degree $-\infty$ under our convention. The putative leading product $2\cdot3$ vanished. This is exactly the step excluded by the domain assumption in Proposition 14.2; convolution itself is still a valid ring multiplication.
