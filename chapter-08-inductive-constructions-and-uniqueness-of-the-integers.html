8. Inductive constructions and uniqueness of the integers

An arbitrary associative multiplication lets us write a long product without parentheses. That permission is a theorem, not typography. Its proof also explains why powers of one element commute even when unrelated elements do not. The same inductive machinery then builds the unique map between two candidate integer systems, proving that the axioms determine their arithmetic rather than merely naming it.

Lessons 17–18 supply the applications and the integer-uniqueness argument. This chapter completes the exercises on finite reordering, negative powers, multiplicativity, and injectivity. It also distinguishes preservation of $1$ by a general operation-preserving map from its automatic preservation by a bijective such map.

8.1 Why every parenthesization agrees Lesson 17 · 17:13

A semigroup is a set with an associative binary operation. No identity or inverses are required. For a nonempty list in a semigroup, recursively define $P_1(x_1)=x_1$ and $P_{n+1}(x_1,\ldots,x_{n+1})=P_n(x_1,\ldots,x_n)x_{n+1}$. Chapter 7's recursion theorem justifies this definition for each sequence of inputs; equivalently the displayed formula defines the finite-product functions successively.

Theorem 8.1, finite associativity, proved here. For $1\le j<n$ we have the splitting identity

$$P_n(x_1,\ldots,x_n)=P_j(x_1,\ldots,x_j)P_{n-j}(x_{j+1},\ldots,x_n).$$

Every full parenthesization of the ordered list therefore has value $P_n$.

Induct on $n$. For $n=2$, the only split is the defining product. Suppose the identity holds for lists of length $n$. For a list of length $n+1$, splitting at $j=n$ is the definition. If $j<n$, use the induction hypothesis on the first $n$ terms:

$$P_{n+1}=(P_jP_{n-j})x_{n+1} =P_j(P_{n-j}x_{n+1})=P_jP_{n+1-j}.$$

The middle equality is associativity of three actual elements. This proves all splits. To obtain the parenthesization claim, induct strongly on list length. Any full parenthesization has an outermost multiplication splitting the list at some $j$. By induction the two parenthesized sublists equal their respective $P$ values; the splitting identity makes their product $P_n$. The length-one base has no multiplication. ∎

This proof also gives uniqueness: any family with the one-term rule and all splitting identities must satisfy the defining last-factor recurrence, so must be $P_n$ by induction. In a monoid define the empty product to be its identity. A semigroup alone has no designated value for an empty product.

Proposition 8.2, finite reordering, proved here. If the semigroup operation is commutative, permuting a finite list leaves its product unchanged.

By Theorem 8.1 an adjacent pair can be isolated by parentheses. Commutativity swaps that pair and reassociation restores the surrounding product. To realize an arbitrary permutation, induct on the length of the list of labeled positions. Move the position wanted first to the front by finitely many adjacent swaps; there are at most $n-1$. Apply the induction hypothesis to the remaining $n-1$ positions. The one-position case is immediate. Each swap preserves the value, so the complete permutation does too, even when some entries coincide. ∎

8.2 Powers and their exact hypotheses Lesson 17 · 00:38

Define $x^1=x$ and $x^{n+1}=x^nx$ in a semigroup. Recursion makes these positive powers unique. In a monoid also set $x^0=e$.

Proposition 8.3, proved here. For positive integers $m,n$, $x^{m+n}=x^mx^n$ and $(x^m)^n=x^{mn}$. In a monoid these equations hold for nonnegative exponents too.

Fix arbitrary $m$ and induct on $n$. The first equation at $n=1$ is the definition. At the next step, $x^{m+n+1}=x^{m+n}x=(x^mx^n)x=x^m(x^nx)=x^mx^{n+1}$. Thus it holds for all $m,n>0$. For the second equation the base is $(x^m)^1=x^m$, and the step is $(x^m)^{n+1}=(x^m)^nx^m=x^{mn}x^m=x^{m(n+1)}$. Zero cases follow from the identity law and $e^n=e$. ∎

Since $m+n=n+m$ in the integers, the first identity gives $x^mx^n=x^nx^m$. This does not imply $xy=yx$ for unrelated $x,y$. Powers remain in the part of the semigroup generated by one element.

In a group define $x^{-r}:=(x^r)^{-1}$ for $r>0$. The inverse of a product is $(ab)^{-1}=b^{-1}a^{-1}$: multiplying in each order gives the identity using associativity and inverse laws. Induction now gives $(x^r)^{-1}=(x^{-1})^r$. Also $x$ commutes with $x^{-1}$ because both products equal $e$, so their finite powers can be interchanged by repeatedly moving adjacent factors past each other.

The sum law holds for all integer exponents. If both exponents are negative, say $-r,-s$, then $x^{-r}x^{-s}=(x^{-1})^{r+s}=x^{-(r+s)}$. For opposite signs, $x^rx^{-s}=x^{r-s}$ when $r\ge s$, because $x^r=x^{r-s}x^s$ and the last factors cancel. When $r<s$, write $(x^s)^{-1}=(x^r x^{s-r})^{-1}=x^{-(s-r)}x^{-r}$ and commute powers of $x$ and $x^{-1}$ to cancel $x^r x^{-r}$. Reversing the mixed factors gives the same value by their commutation. Together with the positive and zero cases these cover every pair of integers.

The power-of-a-power law follows for integer $m$ and nonnegative $n$ by induction using the extended sum law. For $n=-r<0$, $(x^m)^n=((x^m)^r)^{-1}=(x^{mr})^{-1}=x^{-mr}=x^{mn}$. The penultimate equality follows directly from the negative-power definition when $mr>0$, from inverse uniqueness when $mr<0$, and from $e^{-1}=e$ when $mr=0$. Negative powers require invertibility; they are not defined for arbitrary monoid elements.

8.3 Integer multiples inside a ring Lesson 17 · 12:02

In an abelian group written additively, define $nx$ to be the sum of $n$ copies of $x$ for $n>0$, $0x=0$, and $(-n)x=-(nx)$. This is an integer action, not an assumed multiplication between the external integer $n$ and a group element. The power laws translate to $(m+n)x=mx+nx$ and $m(nx)=(mn)x$.

In a ring, distribution and induction give $(nx)y=n(xy)$ for $n\ge0$: the zero case uses $0y=0$, and $((n+1)x)y=(nx+x)y=(nx)y+xy$. For negative $n$, the sign laws of Chapter 6 extend the identity. The same argument using the other distributive law gives $x(ny)=n(xy)$. Consequently

$$ (nx)(my)=(nm)(xy),\qquad (nx)^r=n^r x^r\quad(r\ge1).$$

The first follows by applying the two distribution identities; the second follows by induction on $r$, using the first with the next factor $nx$. On the right, $n^r$ is an integer acting on the ring element $x^r$. With the convention $n^0=1$ even for $n=0$, the zero-exponent formula reads $(nx)^0=1\cdot1_R$ and also holds.

In a unital ring, $(n1_R)x=nx=x(n1_R)$, so integer multiples of $1_R$ commute with every element. This explains why integer coefficients can move through a noncommutative product. It does not authorize interchanging $x$ and $y$ themselves.

8.4 Finite distribution and telescoping Lesson 17 · 30:18

Finite sums are defined by the additive product construction. Induction on $n$ extends both distributive laws:

$$a\sum_{i=1}^n b_i=\sum_{i=1}^n ab_i,\qquad \left(\sum_{i=1}^n b_i\right)a=\sum_{i=1}^n b_i a.$$

For example the first holds for one term. Assuming it for $n$, split the $(n+1)$-term sum into its first $n$ terms plus $b_{n+1}$; binary distribution and the induction hypothesis give the next case. The second uses the other binary distributive law in the same two displayed steps. Empty sums are zero, for which the identities were proved in Chapter 6.

Proposition 8.4, telescoping identity, proved here. In any unital ring and for $n\ge0$,

$$(1-x)(1+x+\cdots+x^n)=1-x^{n+1}.$$

Finite distribution expands the product into $(1+x+\cdots+x^n)-(x+x^2+\cdots+x^{n+1})$. The positive-power law identifies $xx^i=x^{i+1}$. All matching terms cancel in the additive abelian group, leaving $1-x^{n+1}$. The case $n=0$ is the same calculation with product $(1-x)1$. ∎

No division by $1-x$ has occurred. If $1-x$ is not invertible, the identity still holds but cannot be solved by multiplying by its reciprocal. The reversed product also gives the same result because $x$ commutes with its own powers. The binomial theorem and its coefficient conventions will be developed with the real-number applications in Chapter 13.

8.5 Absolute value in an ordered domain Lesson 17 · 31:37

For an ordered integral domain define the absolute value by $|x|=x$ if $x\ge0$ and $|x|=-x$ if $x<0$. Then $|x|\ge0$, equality holds exactly at $x=0$, and $-|x|\le x\le|x|$, all directly from the two sign cases.

Proposition 8.5, proved here. We have $|xy|=|x||y|$ and the triangle inequality $|x+y|\le|x|+|y|$. More generally $|x_1+\cdots+x_n|\le|x_1|+\cdots+|x_n|$.

Write $x=\epsilon|x|$ and $y=\delta|y|$, where each sign is $1$ or $-1$; choose $1$ at zero. Then $xy=\epsilon\delta|x||y|$. The product $|x||y|$ is nonnegative, and the absolute value of either it or its negative equals it. Thus $|xy|=|x||y|$.

Add the inequalities $-|x|\le x\le|x|$ and $-|y|\le y\le|y|$ to obtain $-(|x|+|y|)\le x+y\le|x|+|y|$. For $t\ge0$, the two sign cases in the definition give $-t\le z\le t\Rightarrow|z|\le t$. Apply this with $z=x+y$ and $t=|x|+|y|$. Finally induct on $n$: apply the two-term inequality to $(x_1+\cdots+x_n)+x_{n+1}$ and then apply the induction bound to the first sum. The one-term case is equality. ∎

A useful sharper consequence is the reverse triangle inequality $\big||x|-|y|\big|\le|x-y|$. Indeed $|x|=|(x-y)+y|\le|x-y|+|y|$ gives $|x|-|y|\le|x-y|$. Interchanging $x,y$ gives the opposite bound, since $|y-x|=|x-y|$. The same two-sided characterization of absolute value finishes the proof.

8.6 Determine the map before constructing it Lesson 18 · 07:30

Let $Z,Z'$ be two structures satisfying Chapter 7's integer axioms, with positive cones $N,N'$ and respective identities $0,1$ and $0',1'$. An order-preserving ring isomorphism is a bijective unital ring homomorphism $F:Z\to Z'$ such that $a<b$ implies $F(a)<F(b)$. We will prove there is exactly one.

Even if preservation of $1$ were omitted from the definition, bijectivity plus preservation of addition and multiplication would force it. For any $y\in Z'$, choose $x$ with $F(x)=y$. Then $F(1)y=F(1x)=y$ and $yF(1)=F(x1)=y$. Thus $F(1)$ is the identity of $Z'$, hence equals $1'$. This argument works for any two unital rings. It does not apply to a non-surjective operation-preserving map such as Chapter 6's $r\mapsto(r,0)$.

Any prospective isomorphism must satisfy $F(0)=0'$, $F(1)=1'$, $F(n+1)=F(n)+1'$, and $F(-x)=-F(x)$. Two such maps agree on $N$ by induction from $1$, at zero by the first equation, and on negative elements by the last equation. Therefore there can be at most one, before we have constructed any.

8.7 Construct the integer isomorphism Lesson 18 · 26:34

Theorem 8.6, proved here. There is a unique ring isomorphism $F:Z\to Z'$. It preserves the order, and its inverse also preserves the order.

Define the values. Apply recursion in $Z$ with target $N'$ to define $F(1)=1'$ and $F(n+1)=F(n)+1'$ for $n\in N$. The target is closed under this rule because the sum of positive elements is positive. Extend by $F(0)=0'$ and, for $x<0$, $F(x)=-F(-x)$. The sign cases are disjoint and exhaustive, so this defines a function on $Z$. Its definition gives $F(-x)=-F(x)$ for every $x$: the negative case is direct, the positive case follows by applying that definition to $-x$, and the zero case is immediate.

Successors at every integer. The formula $F(x+1)=F(x)+1'$ holds at positive $x$ by recursion and at zero by the initial value. If $x<0$, discreteness gives $x\le-1$ and therefore $u=-x-1\ge0$. The already established nonnegative successor formula gives $F(-x)=F(u)+1'=-F(x+1)+1'$. Replace $F(-x)$ by $-F(x)$ and rearrange to get $F(x+1)=F(x)+1'$.

Addition. Fix arbitrary $a\in Z$. For $b=0$, $F(a+b)=F(a)+F(b)$ is the zero law. Induction on nonnegative $b$ gives

$$F(a+(b+1))=F(a+b)+1'=F(a)+F(b)+1'=F(a)+F(b+1).$$

If $b=-c<0$, apply the established positive-second-argument identity to $(a+b)+c=a$: $F(a)=F(a+b)+F(c)$. Rearranging and using $F(b)=-F(c)$ proves the additive law for negative $b$ as well. Since $a$ was arbitrary, addition is preserved everywhere.

Multiplication. Again fix arbitrary $a$. The case $b=0$ gives $F(a0)=0'=F(a)0'$. If the law holds for nonnegative $b$, then

$$F(a(b+1))=F(ab+a)=F(ab)+F(a)=F(a)F(b)+F(a)=F(a)F(b+1).$$

Here the last equality uses the target distributive law and successor formula. For $b=-c<0$, the source sign law gives $ab=-(ac)$, hence $F(ab)=-F(ac)=-F(a)F(c)=F(a)(-F(c))=F(a)F(b)$. Therefore $F$ is a unital ring homomorphism.

Order and injection. Every positive source element maps to a positive target element by construction. If $a<b$, then $b-a\in N$, so $F(b)-F(a)=F(b-a)>0'$. Thus $F(a)<F(b)$. If $a\ne b$, total order gives one of these strict comparisons, so $F(a)\ne F(b)$. The map is injective.

Surjection. Let $U'=F(N)\subseteq N'$. It contains $1'$. If $y=F(n)\in U'$, then $y+1'=F(n+1)\in U'$. Apply the induction axiom in the target structure to obtain $U'=N'$. Zero is also attained. A negative $y\in Z'$ has $-y\in N'$, so choose $n\in N$ with $F(n)=-y$; then $F(-n)=y$. Every target element is attained. The inverse preserves order because if $F(a)<F(b)$, neither $a=b$ nor $a>b$ is possible by equality or strict preservation, so $a<b$.

Uniqueness. The preceding section already showed that every ring isomorphism must satisfy the defining successor and sign equations. Induction makes it equal to this $F$ on $N$, then on zero and negative elements. ∎

The underlying sets need not be identical. The theorem identifies their structures by a unique map. In this particular problem “canonical” can be made precise as that uniqueness; in other mathematical contexts a canonical construction need not mean that no other isomorphism exists. Neither existence of an integer model from set axioms nor uniqueness of arbitrary ordered domains was proved.

8.8 Reparenthesizing does not reorder

Permutation composition gives a finite, noncommutative model. The program checks every parenthesization of a five-factor product, then explicitly shows that changing factor order may change the result.

def compose(p, q):
    return tuple(p[q[i]] for i in range(3))


def values(word):
    if len(word) == 1:
        return {word[0]}
    result = set()
    for split in range(1, len(word)):
        for left in values(word[:split]):
            for right in values(word[split:]):
                result.add(compose(left, right))
    return result


p, q = (1, 0, 2), (0, 2, 1)
word = (p, q, p, q, p)
assert len(values(word)) == 1
assert compose(p, q) != compose(q, p)
print(len(values(word)))
print(compose(p, q), compose(q, p))
1
(1, 2, 0) (2, 0, 1)

8.9 Problems with complete solutions

Problem 1. A missing commutativity assumption

Must $(xy)^2=x^2y^2$ hold in a group? Give a concrete answer with $x=(12)$ and $y=(23)$ in $S_3$, using rightmost-first composition.

We have $x^2=y^2=e$, so $x^2y^2=e$. But $xy=(123)$ and $(xy)^2=(132)\ne e$. Thus the formula fails. If $xy=yx$, associativity and the permitted interchange give $(xy)^2=xyxy=xxyy=x^2y^2$. Powers of one element commute; the example involves two different elements.

Problem 2. Telescoping at a noninvertible factor

Apply Proposition 8.4 to $x=1$ in a nontrivial ring. Explain why cancelling $1-x$ would be invalid.

Every power of $1$ equals $1$, so the identity reads $0\cdot((n+1)1)=0$. This is true and supplies no value for the sum by division. The factor $1-x$ is zero and is not invertible in a nontrivial ring: $0y=0\ne1$ for all $y$. Cancelling it would turn the equality $0a=0b$ into a false unrestricted cancellation law. The geometric-sum identity is valid even where its familiar quotient form is undefined.

Problem 3. Reverse triangle inequality and distance stability

If $|x-y|\le r$ in an ordered domain, prove $\big||x|-|y|\big|\le r$.

Triangle inequality applied to $x=(x-y)+y$ gives $|x|-|y|\le|x-y|\le r$. Applied to $y=(y-x)+x$, it gives $|y|-|x|\le|y-x|=|x-y|\le r$. Thus $-r\le|x|-|y|\le r$. The premise implies $r\ge0$, and the two sign cases in absolute value now give the required inequality. Taking absolute values changes a value by no more than the original separation.

Problem 4. Surjective ring maps preserve the identity

Let $h:R\to S$ be surjective and preserve addition and multiplication between unital rings, without assuming $h(1_R)=1_S$. Prove this last equality. Is injection needed?

For arbitrary $s\in S$, take $r\in R$ with $h(r)=s$. Then $h(1_R)s=h(1_Rr)=s$ and $s h(1_R)=h(r1_R)=s$. Therefore $h(1_R)$ is a two-sided identity of $S$ and equals $1_S$ by identity uniqueness. No injection was used. Surjectivity is essential to this proof; Chapter 6's inclusion $\mathbb R\times\{0\}\to\mathbb R^2$ preserves both operations but misses the ambient identity.

Problem 5. Every integer is fixed

Let $h:Z\to Z$ be any unital ring homomorphism of an integer structure satisfying Chapter 7. Prove $h$ is the identity without assuming it bijective.

The unit condition gives $h(1)=1$. If $h(n)=n$ for a positive $n$, then $h(n+1)=h(n)+h(1)=n+1$. Induction gives $h(n)=n$ for every positive $n$. Additive preservation gives $h(0)=0$ by cancellation from $h(0)=h(0)+h(0)$ and gives $h(-n)=-h(n)=-n$. Total order partitions $Z$ into positive, zero, and negative elements, so every element is fixed. Bijectivity follows afterward rather than being a premise.

Problem 6. Recover the order from positivity

Suppose a bijective additive map $h:Z\to Z'$ maps $N$ onto $N'$. Prove $a<b$ if and only if $h(a)<h(b)$.

If $a<b$, then $b-a\in N$, so $h(b)-h(a)=h(b-a)\in N'$ and $h(a)<h(b)$. Conversely, if $h(b)-h(a)\in N'$, choose $c\in N$ mapping to this element by the onto-positive-cone hypothesis. Since $h(c)=h(b-a)$ and $h$ is injective, $c=b-a$. Thus $b-a>0$ and $a<b$. The proof requires no multiplication. For ordered additive structures, the positive cone already determines the strict order through differences.

8.10 Sources and boundaries

Lessons 17–18 are the sources for the algebraic applications and the uniqueness theorem. All claims needed in the uniqueness proof are established above, including the multiplication and injectivity arguments assigned as exercises in the source. The finite program checks only its particular permutation examples. Integer-model existence, formal set-theoretic consistency, and the binomial theorem remain outside this chapter's claims.
