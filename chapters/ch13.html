13. Real numbers and completeness

Successive rational bisections can bracket a number whose square should be $2$, but rational arithmetic alone does not guarantee a point in every bracket. Completeness supplies that point. The important separation is between making the brackets shrink, which uses the Archimedean property, and ensuring their intersection exists, which uses the least-upper-bound property.

Lesson 24 develops completeness, nested intervals, the Archimedean property, and a bisection sketch for the square root of two. We complete those proofs and the converse completeness argument. Rational density, the uncountability argument, and the binomial interlude are additional developments connecting this chapter to earlier promises. Existence and uniqueness of a complete ordered field are stated background theorems, not constructed here.

13.1 A bound need not belong to the set Lesson 24 · 09:40

In an ordered field $F$, a set $S$ is bounded above if some $b\in F$ satisfies $s\le b$ for every $s\in S$. Such a $b$ is an upper bound. A supremum, or least upper bound, is an upper bound $u$ such that $u\le b$ for every upper bound $b$. If it exists it is unique: two candidates each lie below the other, hence are equal. Write it $\sup S$.

A maximum is a member of $S$ that is at least every other member; it is then the supremum. A supremum need not be a maximum. For example the open interval $(a,b):=\{x:a<x<b\}$, with $a<b$, has supremum $b$, which is not a member. To verify leastness, any $v<b$ fails to be an upper bound: the midpoint between $\max(a,v)$ and $b$ belongs to the interval and exceeds $v$.

A lower bound and an infimum, or greatest lower bound, reverse the comparisons. If $S$ is nonempty and bounded below in a field with the least-upper-bound property, then $-S=\{-s:s\in S\}$ is nonempty and bounded above. Its supremum gives $\inf S=-\sup(-S)$, as reversing signs exchanges the bound conditions.

An ordered field is complete here if every nonempty subset bounded above has a supremum in the field. This is order completeness. The real numbers $\mathbb R$ are a complete ordered field. Their existence and uniqueness up to order-preserving field isomorphism are assumed as background results.

The nonempty condition is necessary: every field element is an upper bound of the empty set, and no such bound is least because subtracting $1$ gives a smaller one. The axiom does not assert suprema for unbounded sets. Metric completeness and algebraic closure are different concepts; neither is being used as a substitute for this ordered-field axiom.

13.2 The useful local test for a supremum Lesson 24 · 21:04

Proposition 13.1, proved here. An upper bound $u$ of a nonempty set $S$ is its supremum exactly when, for every $\epsilon>0$, some $s\in S$ satisfies $u-\epsilon<s\le u$.

If $u=\sup S$, the smaller value $u-\epsilon$ cannot be an upper bound, so there is $s\in S$ greater than it; the upper-bound property gives $s\le u$. Conversely, if some upper bound $v$ were smaller than $u$, choose $\epsilon=u-v>0$. The asserted $s$ would satisfy $v=u-\epsilon<s$, contradicting the upper-bound property of $v$. Therefore $u$ lies below every upper bound and is the supremum. ∎

The quantifier is “for every positive $\epsilon$,” with $s$ allowed to depend on $\epsilon$. It does not say there is one member greater than all smaller numbers. Also the upper-bound condition is indispensable: points near a candidate from below do not exclude members above it.

A related observation needs no completeness. If $|x-y|<\epsilon$ for every positive field element $\epsilon$, then $x=y$. Otherwise $d=|x-y|>0$; choosing $\epsilon=d/2$ would assert $d<d/2$, impossible. In a general ordered field, testing only $\epsilon=1/n$ is a stronger issue because those numbers need not be smaller than every positive field element. We establish that property under completeness next.

13.3 Nested closed intervals have a common point Lesson 24 · 30:06

Write $[a,b]=\{x:a\le x\le b\}$. We consider bounded closed intervals with endpoints in $F$ and $a\le b$, so each is nonempty. A sequence is nested when $[a_{n+1},b_{n+1}]\subseteq[a_n,b_n]$ for every $n\ge1$. This implies $a_n\le a_{n+1}\le b_{n+1}\le b_n$.

Theorem 13.2, proved here. In a complete ordered field, the intersection of every nested sequence of nonempty bounded closed intervals is nonempty.

Let $A=\{a_n:n\in\mathbb N\}$. Each $b_j$ bounds all of $A$: if $n\le j$, then $a_n\le a_j\le b_j$; if $n\ge j$, nesting gives $a_n\le b_n\le b_j$. Hence $A$ is nonempty and bounded above. Let $u=\sup A$. For every $j$, $a_j\le u$ because $u$ bounds $A$, and $u\le b_j$ because $b_j$ is one of its upper bounds. Thus $u\in[a_j,b_j]$ for all $j$. ∎

Closedness and boundedness are real hypotheses. The nested open intervals $(0,1/n)$ have no common real point, once the reciprocal estimate below is known. The nested closed but unbounded rays $[n,\infty)$ also have empty intersection. Constant intervals show that the theorem does not guarantee exactly one common point.

13.4 No positive scale escapes integer multiples Lesson 24 · 38:26

Theorem 13.3, Archimedean property, proved here. For positive $x,y$ in a complete ordered field, some $n\in\mathbb N$ satisfies $nx>y$.

If not, $S=\{nx:n\in\mathbb N\}$ would be nonempty and bounded above by $y$. Let $u=\sup S$. Proposition 13.1 with $\epsilon=x$ gives $nx>u-x$ for some $n$. Then $(n+1)x>u$, contradicting that $u$ bounds $S$. ∎

The integer action $nx$ is repeated addition, agreeing with multiplication by the integer copy embedded in the field in Chapter 12. Taking $x=1$ proves that $\mathbb N$ is unbounded above. For any $\epsilon>0$, choose $N>1/\epsilon$. For all $n\ge N$, reciprocal order gives $1/n\le1/N<\epsilon$. This is the precise meaning of $1/n\to0$.

Likewise $2^n\ge n+1$ for $n\ge0$: the base is equality, and $2^{n+1}\ge2(n+1)\ge n+2$. Thus $0<2^{-n}\le1/(n+1)$, so $2^{-n}\to0$ in the same epsilon sense. Multiplying by any fixed positive width preserves eventual smallness by replacing $\epsilon$ with $\epsilon$ divided by that width.

13.5 Shrinking makes the common point unique Lesson 24 · 48:18

Theorem 13.4, proved here. Suppose nested nonempty bounded closed intervals have a common point and, for each positive integer $n$, some interval has width less than $1/n$. In an Archimedean ordered field their intersection has exactly one point.

If two distinct points $u,v$ belonged to every interval, their distance $d=|u-v|$ would be positive. The reciprocal estimate gives $1/n<d$ for some $n$. Choose an interval of width less than $1/n$. Two points in $[a,b]$ have distance at most $b-a$, so this interval cannot contain both $u,v$, a contradiction. Existence was a hypothesis here; uniqueness is the new conclusion. ∎

Combining Theorems 13.2–13.4 gives existence and uniqueness for shrinking nested intervals in a complete ordered field. The logical roles remain separate: interval intersection supplies existence; sufficiently small widths and the Archimedean estimate supply uniqueness.

13.6 A square root constructed by rational bisection Lesson 24 · 55:35

Proposition 13.5, proved here. A complete ordered field contains a unique positive $c$ with $c^2=2$.

Start with $[a_0,b_0]=[1,2]$. Given endpoints satisfying $a_n^2\le2\le b_n^2$, let $m=(a_n+b_n)/2$. If $m^2\le2$, retain $[m,b_n]$; otherwise retain $[a_n,m]$. The new interval is nonempty, nested, has half the width, and preserves the square inequalities. Recursion defines all intervals. Their widths are $2^{-n}$, which become smaller than every positive number. The nested and shrinking results yield a unique common $c\in[1,2]$.

Because squaring is increasing on nonnegative elements, both $c^2$ and $2$ lie between $a_n^2$ and $b_n^2$. Thus

$$|c^2-2|\le b_n^2-a_n^2=(b_n-a_n)(b_n+a_n)\le4\cdot2^{-n}.$$

The right side becomes smaller than every positive $\epsilon$, so Proposition 13.1's related distance observation forces $c^2=2$. This proves the equation without presupposing continuity of squaring or existence of a root. If positive $c,d$ both square to $2$, then $(c-d)(c+d)=0$; $c+d>0$, so domain cancellation gives $c=d$. ∎

The bisection choices reveal the binary digits of the root. Each finite bracket has rational endpoints, yet completeness places a point in all brackets simultaneously. No finite decimal approximation proves the exact equation; the error estimate for every width does.

13.7 Why rational numbers fail completeness

No rational number squares to $2$. Write a hypothetical positive root as $m/n$ in reduced form with $n>0$. Then $m^2=2n^2$. Euclid's lemma for the prime $2$ implies $2\mid m$, so $m=2k$ and $n^2=2k^2$. It follows that $2\mid n$, contradicting coprimality. This proof relies on the reduced-form theorem, not on the appearance of a decimal expansion.

To exhibit a missing supremum inside $\mathbb Q$, let $S=\{q\in\mathbb Q:q\ge0,\ q^2<2\}$. It contains $1$ and is bounded above by $2$. Suppose a rational supremum $u$ exists; then $1\le u\le2$. If $u^2<2$, choose

$$\delta=\min\left(1,\frac{2-u^2}{2(2u+1)}\right)>0.$$

Then $(u+\delta)^2-u^2=\delta(2u+\delta)\le\delta(2u+1)\le(2-u^2)/2$, so $u+\delta$ is a member of $S$ larger than its upper bound $u$, a contradiction. If $u^2>2$, take $\delta=\min(u/2,(u^2-2)/(4u))>0$. We have $u-\delta>0$ and $(u-\delta)^2\ge u^2-2u\delta\ge(u^2+2)/2>2$. Every nonnegative $q\ge u-\delta$ then has $q^2>2$, so $u-\delta$ is a smaller upper bound of $S$, again a contradiction. Finally $u^2=2$ contradicts irrationality. Thus $S$ has no rational supremum.

The rational field is Archimedean: for positive $x,y\in\mathbb Q$, the positive rational $y/x$ is smaller than some integer, as is immediate from its positive-denominator representation, and that integer multiple of $x$ exceeds $y$. Hence being Archimedean is insufficient for completeness. Density of rational points, proved below, is also insufficient.

13.8 Recover completeness from interval intersection Lesson 24 · 65:22

Theorem 13.6, proved here. An ordered field is complete if and only if it is Archimedean and every nested sequence of nonempty bounded closed intervals has nonempty intersection.

The forward direction is Theorems 13.2–13.3. For the converse take a nonempty bounded-above $S$. Choose $s_0\in S$ and an upper bound $b_0$, and put $a_0=s_0-1$. Thus $a_0<b_0$, $a_0$ is not an upper bound, and $b_0$ is. Bisect $[a_0,b_0]$. If the midpoint is an upper bound, use it as the new right endpoint; otherwise use it as the new left endpoint. The intervals remain nested, with a left endpoint that is not an upper bound, a right endpoint that is an upper bound, and widths $(b_0-a_0)2^{-n}$ tending to zero by the Archimedean property.

The assumed interval property and shrinking uniqueness give a common point $u$. If some $s\in S$ exceeded $u$, choose an interval of width less than $s-u$. Its right endpoint satisfies $b_n\le u+(b_n-a_n)<s$, contradicting its upper-bound property. Thus $u$ is an upper bound. If $v<u$, choose width less than $u-v$. Then $a_n\ge u-(b_n-a_n)>v$. Since $a_n$ is not an upper bound, some $s\in S$ exceeds $a_n$, and therefore exceeds $v$. So $v$ is not an upper bound. This proves $u$ is the least upper bound. ∎

The initial left endpoint is chosen strictly below an actual member, rather than merely somewhere below an upper bound. That establishes the non-upper-bound invariant needed in the final minimality argument.

13.9 Rational density and real uncountability

The rational numbers are dense in $\mathbb R$: between any $x<y$ there is a rational number. Choose $n\in\mathbb N$ with $1/n<y-x$. There is a least integer $m>nx$. To justify this, Archimedeanness supplies an integer $M>|nx|+1$, so the integers greater than $nx$ form a nonempty set bounded below by $-M$; translating by $M+1$ reduces its least-element question to well-ordering in $\mathbb N$. Minimality gives $m-1\le nx<m$, whence $x<m/n\le x+1/n<y$.

Nevertheless $\mathbb R$ is uncountable. Given a proposed listing $x_1,x_2,\ldots$, begin with $[0,1]$. At step $n$, its left closed third and right closed third are disjoint nonempty intervals, so at least one excludes $x_n$. Retain the left third if it excludes $x_n$; otherwise retain the right. This defines nested nonempty closed intervals of positive width. Their intersection contains a point by completeness. That point is excluded from $x_n$ at the $n$th stage for every $n$, so it is absent from the listing. Hence no surjection $\mathbb N\to\mathbb R$ exists, and Chapter 9's listing criterion proves uncountability.

The argument does not rely on potentially ambiguous decimal expansions. It exhibits the same diagonal strategy as Cantor's power-set theorem through a geometric exclusion: reserve a surviving interval while defeating the next proposed entry.

13.10 An algebraic interlude: the promised binomial identity

The finite identities anticipated in Chapter 8 require no completeness. Define integers $C(n,k)$ for $n\ge0$ by $C(0,0)=1$, by $C(n,k)=0$ outside $0\le k\le n$, and recursively by $C(n+1,k)=C(n,k-1)+C(n,k)$. These are the binomial coefficients, also written $\binom nk$. The recursion makes each row finite and uniquely determined.

Proposition 13.7, proved here. In a commutative unital ring, for $n\ge0$,

$$(a+b)^n=\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk a^k b^{n-k}.$$

At $n=0$ the single term is $1$. Multiply the asserted identity at $n$ by $a+b$ and distribute. The part multiplied by $a$ contributes coefficient $C(n,k-1)$ to $a^k b^{n+1-k}$; the part multiplied by $b$ contributes $C(n,k)$. Commutativity permits collecting these like products, and Chapter 8's integer action handles the coefficients. Their sum is $C(n+1,k)$ by the defining recursion, including the boundary coefficients because outside values are zero. This proves the next row by induction. ∎

Counting $k$-element subsets of an $n$-element set gives the same recursion: separate subsets according to whether they contain the last element. This supplies the combinatorial interpretation without dividing by factorials in an arbitrary ring.

13.11 A finite bracket with a rigorous error certificate

from fractions import Fraction

left, right = Fraction(1), Fraction(2)
for step in range(32):
    middle = (left + right) / 2
    if middle * middle <= 2:
        left = middle
    else:
        right = middle
    assert left * left <= 2 <= right * right
assert right - left == Fraction(1, 2 ** 32)
print(left, right)

13.12 Problems with complete solutions

Problem 1. Supremum versus maximum

Find the supremum, infimum, maximum, and minimum of $S=\{1-1/n:n\in\mathbb N\}$.

Every value lies in $[0,1)$, and $n=1$ gives $0$, so the minimum and infimum are $0$. The number $1$ is an upper bound. For any $\epsilon>0$, choose $n$ with $1/n<\epsilon$; then $1-\epsilon<1-1/n<1$, so the supremum test gives $\sup S=1$. No term equals $1$, and every term is smaller than the next since $1/(n+1)<1/n$. Hence there is no maximum.

Problem 2. A missing upper-bound hypothesis

A set has members arbitrarily close to $u$ from below. Must $u$ be its supremum?

No. Let $S=(0,1)\cup\{2\}$ and $u=1$. For every $\epsilon>0$, the midpoint between $\max(0,1-\epsilon)$ and $1$ lies in $S$ and exceeds $1-\epsilon$. But $2\in S$ exceeds $u$, so $u$ is not even an upper bound. The actual maximum and supremum are $2$. Both clauses of Proposition 13.1 are needed.

Problem 3. Two empty intersections

Prove $\bigcap_{n\ge1}(0,1/n)=\varnothing$ and $\bigcap_{n\ge1}[n,\infty)=\varnothing$ in $\mathbb R$.

A point in the first intersection would have $x>0$ and $x<1/n$ for every $n$. Choose $n$ with $1/n<x$ to get a contradiction. A point in the second would satisfy $x\ge n$ for every positive integer, contradicting unboundedness of $\mathbb N$. Thus the first example violates closedness, and the second violates boundedness of the intervals, as required by Theorem 13.2.

Problem 4. Existence is a separate obligation

Explain why a nested sequence of rational closed intervals bracketing $\sqrt2$ has empty intersection in $\mathbb Q$, although the widths tend to zero.

Use Proposition 13.5's rational bisection endpoints but regard every interval as a subset of $\mathbb Q$. If a rational $q$ lay in all of them, the same inequalities would give $|q^2-2|\le4\cdot2^{-n}$ for every $n$. The rational field is Archimedean, so this forces $q^2=2$, contradicting irrationality. Thus shrinking guarantees at most one point here, but cannot produce a point. The missing hypothesis is the interval intersection property, equivalently completeness together with the already available Archimedean property.

Problem 5. Scale a supremum

If $S$ is nonempty and bounded above and $c>0$, prove $\sup(cS)=c\sup S$, where $cS=\{cs:s\in S\}$.

Let $u=\sup S$. Then $s\le u$ implies $cs\le cu$, so $cu$ bounds $cS$. For $\epsilon>0$, the supremum criterion with $\epsilon/c>0$ gives $s\in S$ such that $u-\epsilon/c<s$. Multiplication by $c$ yields $cu-\epsilon<cs\le cu$. The same criterion therefore proves $cu=\sup(cS)$. Positivity controls both the inequality direction and the positive tolerance.

Problem 6. Do binomial coefficients require real numbers?

Expand $(a+b)^3$ in a commutative ring satisfying $1+1=0$.

The recursive coefficient row is $1,3,3,1$, so the theorem gives $a^3+3a^2b+3ab^2+b^3$. In this ring $2x=(1+1)x=0$, hence $3x=x$ for every $x$. The result is $a^3+a^2b+ab^2+b^3$. Completeness and order were not used; indeed such a nontrivial field cannot be ordered compatibly. Integer coefficients act by repeated addition and must be interpreted in the target ring.
