2. Proofs, counterexamples, and set identities

A selection rule accepts a record when it passes test $A$ and at least one of tests $B$ and $C$. Rewriting the rule as “passes $A$ and $B$, or passes $C$” accidentally exempts the second branch from test $A$. A proof of equivalence must exclude exactly this kind of unnoticed change. The same methods that verify a selection rule will verify much more abstract algebraic constructions.

Official lessons 4–6 supply the order: axiomatic reasoning and equivalence, methods of proof, then set operations and identities. The selection-rule example, calculations, and exercises are original. We use elementary school facts about integers explicitly where needed; their deeper structure is developed in Chapters 7–11.

2.1 Assumptions and valid inference Lesson 4 · 01:19

An axiom is a statement taken as a starting assumption in a mathematical theory. A hypothesis is an assumption attached to the particular claim being proved. A deduction is valid when the conclusion follows from the assumptions by the accepted inference rules. Validity alone does not guarantee that every assumption describes the intended application.

The rule modus ponens says that from $p$ and $p\Rightarrow q$, infer $q$. Its justification follows from the implication table: if $p$ is true while $q$ is false, the implication would be false. Thus there is no truth assignment making both premises true and the conclusion false.

A direct proof of $p\Rightarrow q$ begins by assuming $p$ and derives $q$. The final claim is conditional: it does not establish $p$ without further information. For example, assume integers $a$ and $b$ are even. Write $a=2u$ and $b=2v$ with integers $u,v$. Then $a+b=2(u+v)$, so the sum is even. The proof uses the definition of evenness and closure of integer addition; it does not rely on a favorable numerical sample.

Ordinary mathematical prose condenses elementary inferences but must preserve their justification. A formal derivation records a specified syntax and explicit inference rules. To see the difference on a very small example, consider the following two axiom schemata, meaning patterns in which $p,q,r$ can be replaced uniformly by arbitrary propositions:

$$\mathrm{H1}:\quad p\Rightarrow(q\Rightarrow p),\qquad \mathrm{H2}:\quad [p\Rightarrow(q\Rightarrow r)]\Rightarrow[(p\Rightarrow q)\Rightarrow(p\Rightarrow r)].$$

Both are tautologies. H1 preserves an already true $p$ under any extra assumption $q$. For H2, if $p$ holds, its two premises first provide $q$ and then provide $q\Rightarrow r$, yielding $r$ by modus ponens. A complete truth table gives the same conclusion.

Table 2.1. A formal derivation of $p\Rightarrow p$ using these schemata.
LineStatementJustification
1$p\Rightarrow((p\Rightarrow p)\Rightarrow p)$H1, with $q$ replaced by $p\Rightarrow p$
2$p\Rightarrow(p\Rightarrow p)$H1, with $q$ replaced by $p$
3$[p\Rightarrow((p\Rightarrow p)\Rightarrow p)]\Rightarrow([p\Rightarrow(p\Rightarrow p)]\Rightarrow[p\Rightarrow p])$H2, with $q=p\Rightarrow p$ and $r=p$
4$[p\Rightarrow(p\Rightarrow p)]\Rightarrow[p\Rightarrow p]$Modus ponens on lines 1 and 3
5$p\Rightarrow p$Modus ponens on lines 2 and 4

The readable argument is shorter: assume $p$; that same assumption gives $p$. Table 2.1 does a different job by making substitution and inference explicit. We do not prove a completeness theorem for a full logical calculus here, nor claim that these two schemata alone express all classical reasoning. The lecture's discussion of formal proof systems motivates this distinction; formal verification of the book is outside the present workflow.

Implication and negation can also express the other two connectives: $p\lor q\Leftrightarrow(\neg p\Rightarrow q)$ and $p\land q\Leftrightarrow\neg(p\Rightarrow\neg q)$. For the first, if $p$ is true both sides are true; if $p$ is false both have the value of $q$. For the second, the implication inside the negation is false exactly when $p$ is true and $q$ is true, so its negation has precisely the conjunction's truth conditions. These are identities of truth values under every assignment, not additional assumptions about $p,q$.

2.2 Equivalence requires the missing directions Lesson 4 · 13:07

To prove that two propositions $p$ and $q$ are equivalent, establish both $p\Rightarrow q$ and $q\Rightarrow p$. In our selection rule, equivalence means that every record accepted by the old rule is accepted by the new one and conversely. A rewrite that only enlarges the accepted set proves one inclusion, not equality.

Proposition 2.1, proved here. If $p\Rightarrow q$, $q\Rightarrow r$, and $r\Rightarrow p$ all hold, then $p,q,r$ are pairwise equivalent.

The three given arrows form a cycle. Starting from $p$, the first arrow gives $q$ and the second gives $r$, proving $p\Rightarrow r$. Starting from $q$, the second and third give $p$, proving $q\Rightarrow p$. Starting from $r$, the third and first give $q$, proving $r\Rightarrow q$. Together with the three hypotheses these are all six implications between distinct propositions. Each pair therefore satisfies its biconditional. ∎

The mechanism generalizes to any finite cycle of implications: follow the arrows from one proposition until reaching another. A chain without a return path is insufficient. Knowing $p\Rightarrow q\Rightarrow r$ does not let us return from $r$ to $p$; the truth assignment $p=\mathrm F$, $q=\mathrm F$, $r=\mathrm T$ satisfies the chain and refutes the return implication.

A proof can also use a chain of biconditionals. Every step must preserve truth in both directions. Squaring an equation often preserves only one direction: $x=1$ implies $x^2=1$, but the latter also permits $x=-1$. When transforming a problem, identify which operations are reversible before writing equivalence symbols.

2.3 Contradiction and a parity obstruction Lesson 5 · 00:44

In a proof by contradiction, assume the negation of the desired claim together with the standing assumptions. Derive both a statement and its negation. If the standing assumptions are consistent, the added negation cannot hold; classical double negation then gives the claim.

Two logical principles should be distinguished. Noncontradiction says $\neg(p\land\neg p)$: a proposition and its negation cannot both hold. Excluded middle says $p\lor\neg p$: one of them holds. Classical contradiction reasoning uses the ordinary classical rules, including the double-negation step. The two named principles are not interchangeable definitions.

A small arithmetic obstruction illustrates the method. We use the school facts that an integer is either even or of the form $2k+1$, and that a rational number has a representation $a/b$ with $b\ne0$ and no common integer factor greater than one. If $n=2k+1$ is odd, then

$$n^2=4k^2+4k+1=2(2k^2+2k)+1,$$

which is odd. Thus an even integer square must have an even integer as its base. The decisive fact is preservation of oddness under squaring, not an approximate square-root computation.

Proposition 2.2, proved here under these elementary integer facts. No rational number $x$ satisfies $x^2=2$.

Suppose such a rational $x$ exists. Write $x=a/b$ in reduced form, with integers $a,b$ and $b\ne0$. The equation gives $a^2=2b^2$, so $a^2$ is even and the parity argument forces $a=2c$ for an integer $c$. Substitution yields $4c^2=2b^2$, hence $b^2=2c^2$. The same parity argument forces $b$ even. Then $2$ divides both numerator and denominator, contradicting reduced form. Therefore no such rational exists. ∎

This statement does not presuppose that a real number with square $2$ exists. The completeness of the real numbers will establish existence in Chapter 13. Approximate decimals cannot distinguish a rational from an irrational number: every finite decimal is rational. Hammack §6.1, printed pp. 138–139, develops contradiction and the same parity obstruction; our formulation separates nonexistence in $\mathbb Q$ from existence in a larger field.

2.4 Contrapositive, cases, and symmetry Lesson 5 · 09:30

The contrapositive of $p\Rightarrow q$ is $\neg q\Rightarrow\neg p$. Both fail precisely when $p$ is true and $q$ is false, so they are logically equivalent. A contrapositive proof assumes $\neg q$ and derives $\neg p$. It need not derive a contradiction. This differs from assuming the whole target statement false and searching for incompatible conclusions.

For example, “if $n^2$ is even, then $n$ is even” is efficiently proved by the contrapositive “if $n$ is odd, then $n^2$ is odd.” The calculation in §2.3 proves exactly that. The converse of the original claim also happens to be true, because $(2k)^2=2(2k^2)$, but it needs this separate argument. See Hammack §5.1, printed p. 129, for the corresponding proof template.

A proof by cases establishes a claim separately under hypotheses that together exhaust the permitted possibilities. For integers, even and odd cases are exhaustive. For real numbers, the cases positive and negative omit zero. A complete argument must either include zero or have already excluded it in its hypotheses.

The phrase “without loss of generality” abbreviates a symmetry argument. Suppose a statement about real numbers $a,b$ is unchanged when their names are exchanged. The two cases $a\le b$ and $b\le a$ cover every pair, and exchanging names converts the second case into the first. Proving the first case suffices only because the hypotheses and desired conclusion survive that exchange.

For example, to prove that $\max(a,b)+\min(a,b)=a+b$, we may assume $a\le b$: then the left side is $b+a$. Exchanging names handles the other case. In contrast, an assertion about $a-b$ is not automatically invariant under exchange; it changes sign. Symmetry must be checked rather than inferred from the similar appearance of two variables.

2.5 Set operations turn logical conditions into objects Lesson 6 · 00:21

Fix an ambient set $U$ and subsets $A,B,C\subseteq U$. Their union $A\cup B$ contains elements belonging to at least one set; their intersection $A\cap B$ contains elements belonging to both. Their difference $A\setminus B$ contains elements of $A$ that do not belong to $B$. The complement $A^c:=U\setminus A$ is always relative to this specified $U$.

For instance, let $U=\{1,2,3,4\}$, $A=\{1,2\}$, $B=\{2,3\}$, and $C=\{4\}$. Then $A\cup B=\{1,2,3\}$, $A\cap B=\{2\}$, $A\setminus B=\{1\}$, and $A^c=\{3,4\}$. Enlarging $U$ changes the complement even when $A$ itself stays fixed. The other three operations depend only on their displayed input sets.

Set identities can be proved by translating membership into logic. For any fixed element $x\in U$, let $p,q,r$ mean $x\in A,x\in B,x\in C$. A union becomes an “or,” an intersection an “and,” and a complement a negation. If the two membership conditions agree for every $x$, extensionality proves equality.

Table 2.2. Basic identities, with their elementwise reasons.

$A\cup U=U$, $A\cap\varnothing=\varnothing$$p\lor\mathrm T$ is true; $p\land\mathrm F$ is false
$A\cup B=B\cup A$, $A\cap B=B\cap A$“At least one” and “both” are unchanged by exchange

These are proofs of the identities, not a numerical confirmation of one example: the fixed element is arbitrary, and the stated logical equivalences hold under every truth assignment. The identities concern sets; they do not say that a union of ordered lists preserves order or repeated entries.

2.6 A correct rule expansion Lesson 6 · 09:35

Proposition 2.3, proved here. For all subsets $A,B,C$ of $U$,

$$A\cap(B\cup C)=(A\cap B)\cup(A\cap C),\qquad A\cup(B\cap C)=(A\cup B)\cap(A\cup C).$$

For the first identity, an element belongs to the left side precisely when it belongs to $A$ and belongs to at least one of $B,C$. If it belongs to $B$, it belongs to $A\cap B$; if it belongs to $C$, it belongs to $A\cap C$. This gives inclusion in the right side. Conversely, membership in either $A\cap B$ or $A\cap C$ supplies membership in $A$ and in at least one of $B,C$, giving membership in the left side.

For the second identity, membership in $A$ supplies membership in both unions on the right. Membership in both $B,C$ does the same. Conversely, suppose an element belongs to both $A\cup B$ and $A\cup C$. If it belongs to $A$, it belongs to the left side. If it does not belong to $A$, the two union memberships force membership in $B$ and $C$, again putting it on the left. Both inclusions hold in each case. ∎

The first identity repairs the opening specification: a record passing $C$ must still pass $A$. With $A=\{1\}$, $B=\varnothing$, and $C=\{2\}$, the correct set $A\cap(B\cup C)$ is empty. The faulty expression $(A\cap B)\cup C$ instead contains $2$. The missing factor is exposed by an element that passes the second branch while failing the common requirement.

One valid implication can still be useful: $A\cap(B\cup C)\subseteq(A\cap B)\cup C$ always holds. It describes an enlargement of the accepted set, not an equivalent rewrite. Naming this relation avoids treating every approximation as an identity. Hammack §8.3, printed pp. 164–165, shows how elementwise equivalences and double inclusions establish set identities.

2.7 Negating a composite filter Lesson 6 · 11:43

Proposition 2.4, proved here. Relative to $U$,

$$ (A\cup B)^c=A^c\cap B^c,\qquad (A\cap B)^c=A^c\cup B^c,\qquad (A^c)^c=A.$$

For $x\in U$, failing membership in $A\cup B$ means belonging to neither $A$ nor $B$. This is membership in both complements, proving the first identity in both directions. Failing membership in $A\cap B$ means that at least one membership condition fails, which is membership in the union of the complements. Finally, failing to be outside $A$ is exactly membership in $A$, by double negation. Every step is reversible for each $x\in U$, so extensionality supplies all three equalities. ∎

The ambient set can be replaced by a displayed set $D$ without assuming that $A$ and $B$ are subsets of it. Then

$$D\setminus(A\cup B)=(D\setminus A)\cap(D\setminus B),\qquad D\setminus(A\cap B)=(D\setminus A)\cup(D\setminus B).$$

For the first, both sides mean “belongs to $D$, belongs to neither $A$ nor $B$.” For the second, both mean “belongs to $D$, and fails at least one of $A,B$.” These membership descriptions prove both formulas and make the role of $D$ explicit.

Returning to the selection rule, rejected records are $[A\cap(B\cup C)]^c=A^c\cup(B^c\cap C^c)$. A record fails either because it fails the common requirement or because it fails both alternatives. The two distinct failure modes are easier to diagnose than an undifferentiated “rule returned false.”

2.8 Exhaustively test a finite set algebra

The program lists all subsets of a three-element universe and tests every triple. Inputs are fixed finite sets; the output is a count of checked triples and a counterexample to the incorrect rewrite. Assertions stop execution if an identity fails. There are no file or network effects. The earlier elementwise proofs establish the universal laws; this finite test checks their implementation and constructs a diagnostic example.

from itertools import combinations, product

universe = {0, 1, 2}
subsets = [set(c) for k in range(4)
           for c in combinations(sorted(universe), k)]
checked = 0
for a, b, c in product(subsets, repeat=3):
    assert a & (b | c) == (a & b) | (a & c)
    assert a | (b & c) == (a | b) & (a | c)
    assert universe - (a | b) == (universe - a) & (universe - b)
    assert universe - (a & b) == (universe - a) | (universe - b)
    checked += 1
assert {0} & (set() | {1}) != ({0} & set()) | {1}
print("subset triples checked:", checked)
print("buggy rewrite:", sorted(({0} & set()) | {1}))

subset triples checked: 512
buggy rewrite: [1]

2.9 Problems and worked solutions

Problem 1 · Validate the formal derivation

Verify the substitution used in line 3 of Table 2.1. Explain why line 4 follows and why line 2 alone cannot justify the last line by modus ponens.

Solution

In H2 replace $q$ by $(p\Rightarrow p)$ and $r$ by $p$. The first bracket becomes $p\Rightarrow((p\Rightarrow p)\Rightarrow p)$, and the second becomes $(p\Rightarrow(p\Rightarrow p))\Rightarrow(p\Rightarrow p)$, exactly line 3. Line 1 is the antecedent of line 3, so modus ponens yields line 4. Line 2 has antecedent $p$; using it directly by modus ponens would require $p$ as another premise, which is not given in this derivation. Instead line 2 is the whole antecedent of line 4, and that pair yields line 5.

Problem 2 · A broken equivalence loop

A proof establishes $p\Rightarrow q$ and $q\Rightarrow r$ but only $p\Rightarrow r$ as its third step. Does it prove equivalence? Repair the implication diagram with one additional direction.

Solution

No. The third step follows from the first two and adds no return path. The truth assignment $p=q=\mathrm F$, $r=\mathrm T$ satisfies all three and shows the propositions need not agree. Adding $r\Rightarrow p$ closes the cycle. Following paths then supplies $q\Rightarrow p$ and $r\Rightarrow q$, while the existing chain supplies $p\Rightarrow r$, giving all six implications.

Problem 3 · Remove an existence assumption

Why is “no rational number has square $2$” a more foundational statement than “$\sqrt2$ is irrational” at this point? Identify the two integer facts used in its proof.

Solution

The first statement quantifies only over already familiar rationals. The second names a real square root and thus assumes existence in a larger number system. The parity proof uses that every integer is even or odd and that a rational can be put in reduced form. The odd-square expansion then forces an even square to have an even base, and reduced form rules out the shared factor $2$. Neither fact proves existence of a real square root. That requires additional structure, such as completeness.

Problem 4 · Is the symmetry legitimate?

To prove $(a-b)^2\ge0$ for all real $a,b$, may one assume $a\le b$ without loss of generality? May one use the same reduction to prove $a-b\ge0$ for all real $a,b$?

Solution

The first reduction is legitimate. Swapping $a,b$ replaces $(a-b)^2$ by $(b-a)^2=(a-b)^2$, preserving the statement and its unrestricted hypotheses. The cases $a\le b$ and $b\le a$ exhaust all pairs. The second reduction is not legitimate: swapping changes $a-b$ to its negative and does not preserve the assertion. Indeed $a=0,b=1$ gives $a-b=-1$, refuting the universal claim.

Problem 5 · A relative complement identity

Prove $D\setminus(A\setminus B)=(D\setminus A)\cup(D\cap B)$ without assuming any inclusion between $A,B,D$.

Solution

An element belongs to the left side precisely when it belongs to $D$ and does not satisfy “in $A$ and outside $B$.” By De Morgan and double negation, this means it belongs to $D$ and satisfies “outside $A$ or in $B$.” Distributing the common condition gives “in $D$ and outside $A$, or in $D$ and in $B$,” which is membership in the displayed right side. Every equivalence is reversible for each element, so the sets are equal. No universal complement or containment assumption was used.

Problem 6 · Diagnose the rewrite

Characterize exactly when $(A\cap B)\cup C$ equals $A\cap(B\cup C)$. Find the smallest possible universe on which they differ.

Solution

The first set can be written as $[(A\cap B)\cup(A\cap C)]\cup(C\setminus A)$: split $C$ into its part inside $A$ and its part outside. The bracket is the correct distributive expansion and lies within $A$, while $C\setminus A$ is disjoint from $A$. Equality therefore holds exactly when $C\setminus A=\varnothing$, equivalently $C\subseteq A$. A one-element universe $U=\{u\}$ already gives failure by taking $A=B=\varnothing$ and $C=\{u\}$. The correct expression is empty and the faulty expression is $\{u\}$. The empty universe cannot give a counterexample because all its subsets coincide.
