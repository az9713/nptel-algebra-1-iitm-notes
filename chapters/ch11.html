11. Congruences and prime factorization

Modulo $15$, the equation $6x\equiv9$ has three residue-class solutions, while modulo a prime every nonzero coefficient can be inverted. The difference is controlled by a greatest common divisor. Congruence arithmetic keeps precisely the information needed for these questions and discards the rest, turning infinite integer computations into finite algebra.

Lessons 21–22 introduce congruences, residue systems, Fermat's little theorem, Euler's theorem, and linear congruences, and state Wilson's theorem. The proofs below include both the source's permutation and necklace arguments for Fermat, a full proof of Euler's converse, and an added elementary proof of Wilson. Prime factorization was proved from Lesson 20 in Chapter 10 and is used here to analyze the resulting arithmetic.

11.1 Equality of a chosen kind Lesson 21 · 00:47

Fix a positive integer $n$, called the modulus. Define $a\equiv b\pmod n$ to mean $n\mid a-b$. Equivalently $a=b+kn$ for some integer $k$. This is a relation between two integers, not an assertion that $a=b$ as integers.

It is an equivalence relation: $n\mid0$ proves reflexivity; changing the sign of $a-b$ proves symmetry; and adding $a-b$ and $b-c$ proves transitivity. Its residue class $[a]_n$ is the set $\{a+kn:k\in\mathbb Z\}$. For example $[2]_6$ contains $\ldots,-10,-4,2,8,14,\ldots$.

Proposition 11.1, proved here. Congruence is compatible with addition, subtraction, multiplication, and nonnegative integer powers.

If $a\equiv a'$ and $b\equiv b'$ modulo $n$, then $n$ divides $(a+b)-(a'+b')=(a-a')+(b-b')$ and $(a-b)-(a'-b')=(a-a')-(b-b')$. For multiplication use $ab-a'b'=b(a-a')+a'(b-b')$, also divisible by $n$. Induct on $k$ to obtain $a^k\equiv(a')^k$: at $k=0$ both values are $1$, and the step multiplies the established congruence by $a\equiv a'$. ∎

This proves that replacing a number by a congruent representative preserves the result of any expression built from these operations. Division is absent from the list; it needs an invertibility hypothesis. Negative powers likewise require a specified inverse.

11.2 Exactly n classes, including the zero class Lesson 21 · 20:16

Proposition 11.2, proved here. Two integers are congruent modulo $n$ exactly when their division remainders in $\{0,\ldots,n-1\}$ agree. There are exactly $n$ residue classes.

Write $a=nq+r$ and $b=ns+t$ with $0\le r,t<n$. If $r=t$, their difference is a multiple of $n$. Conversely, if $n\mid a-b$, subtract the multiple $n(q-s)$ to obtain $n\mid r-t$. But $|r-t|<n$; a nonzero multiple of $n$ has absolute value at least $n$, so $r=t$. Every integer therefore belongs to the class of its remainder, and different permitted remainders give different classes. There are $n$ integers from $0$ through $n-1$, including zero. ∎

A complete residue system is a set containing exactly one integer from each class. It is characterized by having $n$ elements, no two congruent. Necessity follows directly from one representative per class. Conversely the remainder map from such a set to $\{0,\ldots,n-1\}$ is injective between two $n$-element sets, so the finite pigeonhole principle makes it surjective. Hence every class is represented.

For example $\{-2,-1,0,1,2\}$ is a complete system modulo $5$ just as $\{0,1,2,3,4\}$ is. Modulo $1$ every integer is congruent to every other, so there is one class, not zero classes.

11.3 A quotient ring and its invertible classes

Write $\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z$ for the set of residue classes and define $[a]_n+[b]_n=[a+b]_n$, $[a]_n[b]_n=[ab]_n$. Proposition 11.1 proves these operations well defined: changing either representative changes neither resulting class. The integer ring identities then pass to classes, because each identity is true of representatives. Thus this is a commutative unital ring, with zero $[0]_n$, identity $[1]_n$, and additive inverse $[-a]_n$. At $n=1$ it is the one-element ring.

Proposition 11.3, proved here. A class $[a]_n$ is a multiplicative unit exactly when $\gcd(a,n)=1$. In particular, if $ac\equiv bc\pmod n$ and $\gcd(c,n)=1$, then $a\equiv b\pmod n$.

If the gcd is $1$, Bézout gives $au+nv=1$, so $[a]_n[u]_n=[1]_n$. Commutativity makes this a two-sided inverse. Conversely an inverse means $au\equiv1\pmod n$ for some integer $u$, so $au-nv=1$ for some $v$. Every common divisor of $a,n$ divides $1$, hence their positive gcd is $1$. For cancellation, multiply both sides by the inverse of $[c]_n$, or apply Chapter 10's coprime cancellation to $n\mid c(a-b)$. ∎

For $n>1$ the coprimality condition is also necessary for universal cancellation. If $d=\gcd(c,n)>1$, then $c(n/d)$ is divisible by $n$, whereas $n/d$ is a positive integer smaller than $n$, so is not congruent to zero. Thus multiplication by $c$ identifies the distinct classes of $0$ and $n/d$. For example $2\cdot1\equiv2\cdot4\pmod6$, but $1\not\equiv4\pmod6$.

11.4 Affine transformations permute residues Lesson 22 · 10:14

Lemma 11.4, proved here. If $\gcd(a,n)=1$, then $[x]_n\mapsto[ax+b]_n$ is a permutation of the $n$ residue classes for every integer $b$.

The operation laws make the map well defined. If two outputs agree, subtract $b$ and cancel $a$ by Proposition 11.3 to obtain equality of the input classes. The map is injective on a finite set and therefore bijective by Chapter 9. Equivalently its inverse is $[y]_n\mapsto[u(y-b)]_n$, where $au\equiv1\pmod n$. ∎

For example modulo $4$, the map $x\mapsto7x-2$ sends $0,1,2,3$ to integers $-2,5,12,19$, with remainders $2,1,0,3$. Each class appears once. The representative values are not required to lie between zero and the modulus.

11.5 Fermat's theorem by a product permutation Lesson 22 · 22:50

Theorem 11.5, Fermat's little theorem, proved here. If $p$ is prime and $p\nmid a$, then $a^{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p$. For every integer $a$, $a^p\equiv a\pmod p$.

Primality and $p\nmid a$ give $\gcd(a,p)=1$. Multiplication by $a$ permutes the residue classes and maps zero only to zero. Hence $a,2a,\ldots,(p-1)a$ have, in some order, the nonzero remainders $1,2,\ldots,p-1$. Multiplying gives

$$a^{p-1}(p-1)!\equiv(p-1)!\pmod p.$$

The prime $p$ divides none of the factors $1,\ldots,p-1$, so by Euclid's lemma it does not divide their product. Thus $\gcd((p-1)!,p)=1$, and cancellation gives the first assertion. Multiplying by $a$ gives the second when $p\nmid a$. If $p\mid a$, both $a^p$ and $a$ are congruent to zero, proving the remaining case. ∎

The cancellation acts on a factor known to be invertible modulo $p$. It is not division by an ordinary factorial within the integers.

11.6 The same congruence from rotating necklaces Lesson 22 · 14:30

Let $a\ge1$ be a number of colors and let $p$ be prime. A coloring of $p$ labeled positions is a word $(c_0,\ldots,c_{p-1})$, with $a^p$ possibilities. Identify words that differ by cyclic rotation only; reflections are not included. Rotation equivalence partitions the words into disjoint classes.

There are $a$ monochromatic words, each fixed by every rotation. Every other class has exactly $p$ words. To prove this, suppose two of its $p$ rotations agree. Their difference is a shift by $k$ positions, with $1\le k<p$, fixing the word. Since $\gcd(k,p)=1$, the positions $0,k,2k,\ldots,(p-1)k$ modulo $p$ are all distinct by modular cancellation, hence exhaust all positions. Repeated invariance under this shift makes every color equal to the color at zero, contradicting non-monochromaticity. Thus all $p$ rotations are distinct.

The non-monochromatic words consequently split into equal-size classes of $p$, so $p\mid a^p-a$. This is Fermat's second congruence for positive color counts; every integer is congruent to a number in $\{0,\ldots,p-1\}$, and congruence respects powers, so it extends to arbitrary integers. The residue zero case is immediate. If $p\nmid a$, coprime cancellation of $a$ gives the first congruence. The proof exposes why primality matters: for composite length, a nonconstant repeated pattern can be fixed by a nonzero shift.

11.7 Euler's theorem and its converse Lesson 22 · 29:29

For $n>1$, the Euler totient $\varphi(n)$ is the number of integers $r$ with $1\le r<n$ and $\gcd(r,n)=1$. Set $\varphi(1)=1$ as a convention. A reduced residue system contains one representative of each class that is a unit. Its size is $\varphi(n)$ for $n>1$. The canonical representatives modulo $12$ are $1,5,7,11$, so $\varphi(12)=4$.

Theorem 11.6, Euler's theorem, proved here. For $n>1$ and any integer $a$,

$$a^{\varphi(n)}\equiv1\pmod n\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\gcd(a,n)=1.$$

Assume $a$ is coprime to $n$. Multiplication by its unit class permutes the unit classes: a product of units is a unit, its inverse is obtained by reversing the inverse factors, and multiplication by the inverse of $a$ reverses this map. Choose representatives $r_1,\ldots,r_t$ for them, where $t=\varphi(n)$. Comparing products before and after this permutation gives $a^t r_1\cdots r_t\equiv r_1\cdots r_t\pmod n$. Each $r_i$ is invertible modulo $n$, so their product is invertible; cancel it to obtain $a^t\equiv1$.

Conversely $t\ge1$, since $1$ is one of the counted representatives. If $d$ divides both $a$ and $n$, it divides $a^t$ and, from the congruence, divides $a^t-1$. It therefore divides $1$. The positive gcd must be $1$. ∎

At $n=1$, every congruence holds and $\gcd(a,1)=1$, so the equivalence remains true with our convention. At a prime $p$, every $1\le r<p$ is coprime to $p$, giving $\varphi(p)=p-1$ and recovering Fermat. For a prime power $p^k$, the integers not coprime to it are exactly the multiples of $p$, by unique factorization. Among $1,\ldots,p^k$ there are $p^{k-1}$ of these. Thus $\varphi(p^k)=p^k-p^{k-1}$; counting through $p^k$ instead of stopping before it changes no coprime count.

11.8 Solve every linear congruence Lesson 22 · 35:28

Theorem 11.7, proved here. Let $n>0$, $a,b\in\mathbb Z$, and $d=\gcd(a,n)$. The congruence $ax\equiv b\pmod n$ is solvable exactly when $d\mid b$. When solvable, its integer solutions are one class modulo $n/d$, giving exactly $d$ classes modulo $n$.

The congruence means $ax-nk=b$ for an integer $k$. Every common divisor of $a,n$ then divides $b$, proving necessity. Conversely choose $u,v$ with $au+nv=d$. If $b=dt$, the integer $x_0=ut$ satisfies $ax_0\equiv dt=b\pmod n$.

Write $a=da'$, $n=dn'$. Dividing the Bézout identity by $d$ gives $a'u+n'v=1$, so $a',n'$ are coprime. Two integers $x,x_0$ both solve the congruence exactly when $n\mid a(x-x_0)$, equivalently $n'\mid a'(x-x_0)$. Coprime cancellation makes this equivalent to $n'\mid x-x_0$. Hence all and only solutions are $x=x_0+tn'$ for $t\in\mathbb Z$. Modulo $n=dn'$, the values $t=0,\ldots,d-1$ give distinct classes: equality would force $d\mid t-s$, and $|t-s|<d$. Every other $t$ has a remainder among these $d$ possibilities. ∎

The proof also covers $a=0$: then $d=n$, solvability is $n\mid b$, and $n/d=1$, so every integer is a solution. It covers $n=1$ as well. When $d=1$, the solution class is simply the modular inverse of $a$ times $b$.

11.9 Wilson's exact primality criterion Lesson 22 · 45:21

Theorem 11.8, Wilson's theorem, proved here. For an integer $m>1$, $(m-1)!\equiv-1\pmod m$ if and only if $m$ is prime.

If $m=p$ is prime, every nonzero class has a unique inverse by Proposition 11.3 and group inverse uniqueness. Classes not equal to their inverse pair off, each pair contributing product $1$. A class is its own inverse exactly when $x^2\equiv1\pmod p$, or $p\mid(x-1)(x+1)$. Euclid's lemma says these are precisely $[1]_p$ and $[-1]_p$. For $p>2$ they are distinct, and their product is $-1$, so the full nonzero product is $-1$. At $p=2$ the only nonzero class is $1$, which is also $-1$ modulo $2$, giving the same assertion.

Conversely if $m$ is composite, choose a divisor $d$ with $1<d<m$. Then $d$ divides $(m-1)!$ because $d$ occurs as a factor, and $d\mid m$. The proposed congruence would imply that $d$ divides $(m-1)!+1$, hence divides $1$, impossible. Therefore the congruence forces primality. ∎

This criterion is logically exact. Its factorial form alone supplies no claim that it is an efficient way to test large integers. Similarly, passing one Fermat congruence is not a proof of primality; a counterexample appears in the problems.

11.10 Exact residue and necklace experiments

from itertools import product
from math import gcd

solutions = [x for x in range(15) if (6 * x - 9) % 15 == 0]
print(solutions)
units = [r for r in range(1, 30) if gcd(r, 30) == 1]
assert pow(7, len(units), 30) == 1
print(units)

words = set(product(range(3), repeat=5))
sizes = []
while words:
    word = min(words)
    orbit = {word[k:] + word[:k] for k in range(5)}
    sizes.append(len(orbit))
    words.difference_update(orbit)
assert set(sizes) == {1, 5}
print(sizes.count(1), sizes.count(5))
print(pow(2, 340, 341), 11 * 31)
[4, 9, 14]
[1, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19, 23, 29]
3 48
1 341

The $3^5=243$ labeled words decompose into $3$ singleton monochromatic classes and $48$ rotation classes of size $5$, confirming $243-3=5\cdot48$. The final line demonstrates why the converse of Fermat's theorem fails for a fixed base.

11.11 Problems with complete solutions

Problem 1. All three solutions

Solve $6x\equiv9\pmod{15}$ and show your answer is complete.

The gcd is $3$, which divides $9$. Dividing the divisibility equation by $3$ gives $2x\equiv3\pmod5$. The inverse of $2$ modulo $5$ is $3$, so $x\equiv9\equiv4\pmod5$. All integers $x=4+5t$ solve the original congruence by multiplication back by $3$. Modulo $15$ this gives the three classes $4,9,14$, exactly the gcd count. No class is lost by replacing the modulus by $5$ together with the divided coefficients.

Problem 2. An affine permutation

Find the inverse of $x\mapsto3x+5$ on residue classes modulo $8$.

Since $3\cdot3=9\equiv1\pmod8$, solve $y=3x+5$ by multiplying $y-5$ by $3$. This gives $x\equiv3y-15\equiv3y+1\pmod8$. Composition in either order gives $x$ or $y$ because $9\equiv1$ and $16\equiv0$. Thus the inverse is $y\mapsto3y+1$. The integer values of either expression may be reduced modulo $8$ at any stage by Proposition 11.1.

Problem 3. Why digit tests work

Prove the decimal digit-sum test for $9$ and the alternating-digit-sum test for $11$.

Write a nonnegative integer as $N=\sum_{j=0}^k d_j10^j$, with $d_0$ the units digit. Since $10\equiv1\pmod9$, powers give $N\equiv\sum d_j\pmod9$. Therefore $9\mid N$ exactly when it divides the digit sum. Since $10\equiv-1\pmod{11}$, we instead obtain $N\equiv\sum(-1)^j d_j\pmod{11}$. Thus $11\mid N$ exactly when it divides this alternating sum. Starting the alternating sum at the other end changes at most its sign, which does not affect divisibility.

Problem 4. A composite that passes a Fermat test

Show $341$ is composite but satisfies $2^{340}\equiv1\pmod{341}$.

We have $341=11\cdot31$, so it is composite. But $2^{10}=1024=3\cdot341+1$, hence $2^{10}\equiv1$. Raising to the $34$th power gives $2^{340}\equiv1$. This is a base-two Fermat pseudoprime: a composite number coprime to the base that satisfies that base's Fermat congruence. It does not mean that every base must pass, nor does Fermat's forward theorem have an exception.

Problem 5. Compute a totient from prime factors

Find $\varphi(30)$ and use it to evaluate $7^{100}$ modulo $30$.

The prime divisors of $30$ are $2,3,5$. Among $1,\ldots,30$, exclude multiples of these primes. Inclusion and exclusion gives $30-(15+10+6)+(5+3+2)-1=8$: pairwise intersections are multiples of $6,10,15$, and the triple intersection is the single multiple of $30$. These are exactly the integers coprime to $30$ by unique factorization. Euler gives $7^8\equiv1$. Since $100=12\cdot8+4$, $7^{100}\equiv7^4$. Now $7^2=49\equiv19$, and $19^2=361\equiv1$, so the answer is $1$.

Problem 6. Composite necklace lengths

For binary words of length $4$, give a nonconstant word whose rotation class has fewer than four elements. Explain why the prime-length argument excludes this behavior.

The word $0101$ rotates to $1010$ and then back to $0101$, so its class has two members. It is fixed by shifting two positions. Here the shift $2$ is not coprime to the length $4$; iterating it visits only positions $0,2$ from zero and does not force all colors equal. For prime length $p$, every nonzero shift less than $p$ is coprime to $p$ and visits every position, so a fixed word must be monochromatic. That is the precise hypothesis used in the necklace proof.
