19. Spanning, independence, bases, and dimension

A spanning list provides enough building blocks. An independent list introduces no ambiguity. A basis does both, turning each vector into a unique finite list of scalars. The difficult step is proving that the number of coordinates does not depend on which basis we choose. The exchange argument supplies that invariant and makes dimension a theorem-backed measurement.

Lessons 34–35 develop span, linear independence, exchange, bases, complements, and the dimension formula. We supply explicit boundary cases, separate finite support from arbitrary sequences, and prove the dimension criterion for a finite direct sum.

19.1 Enough vectors and no redundant vectors Lesson 34 · 05:00

Fix a vector space $V$ over a field $F$. A finite list $v_1,\ldots,v_m$ is ordered and may repeat entries. Its span consists of all sums $a_1v_1+\cdots+a_mv_m$ with scalars in $F$. Reordering or repeating entries does not change the span: combine coefficients belonging to a repeated vector and transport coefficients along a permutation. The empty list spans $\{0\}$, because its only linear combination is the empty sum $0$.

The span is the smallest subspace containing the entries. It contains zero, and sums and scalar multiples of linear combinations remain linear combinations. Conversely every containing subspace contains each finite linear combination by repeated closure. For an arbitrary subset $S$, define $\operatorname{span}S$ using finite linear combinations of its members; no convergence of infinite series is involved.

The list is linearly independent if $\sum_{j=1}^m a_jv_j=0$ forces every $a_j=0$. Otherwise it is dependent. The empty list is independent: there is no nonzero coefficient to produce a counterexample.

Proposition 19.1, proved here. Independence is equivalent to unique coefficient representation for every vector in the span.

Subtract two representations of the same vector. Their difference is a relation $\sum(a_j-b_j)v_j=0$. Independence forces $a_j=b_j$ for each $j$. Conversely, uniqueness applied to zero compares any zero relation with the all-zero coefficients, forcing the relation to be trivial. ∎

A singleton is independent exactly when its vector is nonzero. A list containing zero or a repeated entry is dependent: use coefficient $1$ on zero, or coefficients $1,-1$ on the repeated positions. These remain nontrivial relations in characteristic two because $1\ne0$. Every sublist of an independent list is independent, since a relation on the sublist extends by zero coefficients to the original list.

19.2 The deletion lemma Lesson 34 · 23:41

Lemma 19.2, proved here. A finite list is dependent if and only if some entry belongs to the span of its predecessors. Deleting any such entry leaves the span unchanged.

For a nontrivial relation, choose the largest index $k$ with $a_k\ne0$. Then

$$v_k=-a_k^{-1}\sum_{j<k}a_jv_j.$$

If $k=1$, this says $v_1=0$, consistent with the empty-span convention. Conversely, an expression for $v_k$ in earlier entries becomes a nontrivial relation by moving $v_k$ to the other side, with coefficient $-1\ne0$. For the deletion assertion, substitute that expression wherever $v_k$ occurs in a linear combination. The deleted list still spans the original span; the reverse inclusion is automatic because it is a sublist. ∎

For example, in $F^3$ the list $e_1,e_2,e_1+e_2,e_3$ spans the whole space. The third vector is the sum of its predecessors; deleting it preserves the span. The remaining three vectors are independent by comparison of their three coordinates. The deletion lemma converts a relation into a useful operation rather than merely announcing dependence.

19.3 Exchange: why coordinate counts are bounded Lesson 35 · 00:57

Theorem 19.3, proved here. If $u_1,\ldots,u_m$ is independent and $w_1,\ldots,w_n$ spans $V$, then $m\le n$. Moreover the independent list can replace $m$ entries of the spanning list while preserving its span.

Start with the spanning list of $n$ entries. Suppose we have replaced $k$ of them by $u_1,\ldots,u_k$, placed at the beginning, and retained a spanning list of length $n$. If $k<m$, insert $u_{k+1}$ immediately after that prefix. The resulting list is dependent because the old list already spans $u_{k+1}$. By Lemma 19.2 some entry is in the span of its predecessors. It cannot be in the independent prefix $u_1,\ldots,u_{k+1}$; hence it is a retained $w$ entry. Delete that entry, preserving the span and restoring length $n$.

This process cannot exhaust the $w$ entries before all $u$ entries have been inserted. If it did, $u_1,\ldots,u_n$ would span $V$, and the next independent vector would lie in their span, contradicting independence. Thus $m\le n$ and the $m$ replacements are possible. If $n=0$, then $V=\{0\}$ and any nonempty independent list is impossible; if $m=0$, no replacements are required. ∎

This proof uses no previously defined dimension, determinants, or rank. Consequently it can serve as the logical foundation for those later invariants. It says both that four vectors in $F^3$ must be dependent and that three vectors cannot span $F^4$, since the standard coordinate lists are respectively spanning and independent.

19.4 Extracting and extending bases Lesson 35 · 23:48

A space is finite-dimensional if it has a finite spanning list. A finite basis is a list that is both independent and spanning. Thus every vector has exactly one expression in a basis. The empty list is a basis of the zero space.

Theorem 19.4, proved here. Every finite spanning list contains a basis. Every finite independent list in a finite-dimensional space extends to a basis.

For extraction, scan a spanning list from left to right, keeping an entry exactly when it is outside the span of the entries already kept. At each step the kept entries span all entries scanned so far: a discarded entry was already in their span. The kept list is independent by Lemma 19.2. At the end it also spans the original list and hence $V$.

For extension, put the given independent list first, followed by a finite spanning list of $V$, and run the same scan. None of the given independent entries is discarded. The resulting basis therefore contains the entire prescribed prefix. Both procedures terminate because the scanned list is finite. ∎

A maximal independent list means one to which no further vector can be added while retaining independence. It must span, since any vector outside its span could be added, by the deletion lemma. A spanning list minimal under deleting entries must be independent, because dependence permits a deletion. Both descriptions therefore characterize bases. They do not say that every spanning list has the same length: a basis with extra repeated entries still spans.

19.5 Dimension is well defined Lesson 35 · 41:41

Theorem 19.5, proved here. Any two finite bases of $V$ have the same length.

Apply exchange with the first basis independent and the second spanning to get $m\le n$. Reverse their roles to get $n\le m$. Hence $m=n$. ∎

We may now define $\dim_F V$ as that common nonnegative integer. The field subscript matters: $\dim_{\mathbb R}\mathbb C=2$ with basis $1,i$, whereas $\dim_{\mathbb C}\mathbb C=1$ with basis $1$. In the real case $a+bi$ is the unique coordinate expression; in the complex case every $z$ is simply $z\cdot1$.

Coordinate comparison proves that $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ is a basis of $F^n$, that the matrix units $E_{ij}$ form a basis of $M_{m,n}(F)$, and that $1,X,\ldots,X^d$ is a basis of the formal polynomial space $P_d(F)$. Their dimensions are $n,mn,d+1$. These polynomial statements hold over finite fields because equality means coefficient equality, not merely equality of evaluated functions.

Corollary 19.6, proved here. In a space of dimension $n$, an independent list of length $n$ is a basis; a spanning list of length $n$ is a basis.

Extend the independent list to a basis. Every basis has length $n$, so there is no room to add an entry. Alternatively extract a basis from the spanning list. Its required length $n$ leaves no entry to discard. The argument includes $n=0$. ∎

The space $F[X]$ is not finite-dimensional. A finite list of polynomials has a common degree bound and cannot span a monomial of higher degree. The space $F^{\mathbb N}$ of all sequences is also not finite-dimensional: for every positive $r$, its first $r$ coordinate sequences are independent, contradicting exchange against any fixed finite spanning list when $r$ is larger. The infinite family of coordinate sequences spans only the finite-support sequences, because linear combinations are finite. It is not a basis of all sequences. We do not develop infinite bases or their choice principles here.

19.6 Subspace dimensions and complements Lesson 35 · 44:36

Theorem 19.7, proved here. If $U\subseteq V$ is a subspace and $V$ is finite-dimensional, then $U$ has a basis, $\dim U\le\dim V$, and equality holds exactly when $U=V$. There exists a subspace $W$ with $V=U\oplus W$.

Build an independent list in $U$, starting empty and appending a vector outside its span whenever possible. Exchange against a finite basis of $V$ prevents more than $\dim V$ appendings, so the process must reach a spanning list of $U$. This is a basis, and exchange gives the dimension inequality. Extend it to a basis $u_1,\ldots,u_k,w_1,\ldots,w_s$ of $V$. If dimensions are equal, $s=0$ and $U=V$; the reverse direction is immediate.

Let $W=\operatorname{span}(w_1,\ldots,w_s)$. The combined basis spans $V=U+W$. If $u\in U\cap W$, expressing $u$ using each block and subtracting gives a relation on the combined basis. All coefficients vanish, so $u=0$. Chapter 18's criterion gives $V=U\oplus W$. In particular $\dim W=\dim V-\dim U$. ∎

Complements need not be unique or perpendicular. In $\mathbb R^2$, every line $\mathbb R(a,1)$ complements the horizontal axis: $(x,y)=(x-ay,0)+y(a,1)$. Different values of $a$ produce different decompositions of the same vector.

19.7 Count shared directions once Lesson 35 · 55:18

Theorem 19.8, proved here. For subspaces $U,W$ of a finite-dimensional space,

$$\dim(U+W)=\dim U+\dim W-\dim(U\cap W).$$

Choose a basis $c_1,\ldots,c_r$ of the intersection and extend it separately to bases $(c_1,\ldots,c_r,u_1,\ldots,u_p)$ of $U$ and $(c_1,\ldots,c_r,w_1,\ldots,w_q)$ of $W$. Their concatenation with the common block included once spans $U+W$.

For independence suppose $\sum a_ic_i+\sum b_ju_j+\sum d_kw_k=0$. The vector $z=\sum b_ju_j$ lies in $U$ and, by moving the other terms, in $W$. Hence $z=\sum e_ic_i$ for some scalars. Independence of the chosen $U$ basis implies every $b_j=0$ and every $e_i=0$. The original relation now involves only the $W$ basis, so every $a_i,d_k$ vanishes. Thus the concatenation is a basis and its length is $r+p+q=(r+p)+(r+q)-r$. Empty blocks cause no change in the argument. ∎

For two subspaces, dimension is additive exactly when their intersection is zero, equivalently when their sum is direct. For finitely many subspaces $U_1,\ldots,U_s$, choose a basis of each and concatenate the lists. The concatenation spans their sum. It is independent exactly when the sum is direct: a zero relation groups into components in the $U_i$, and uniqueness of the zero decomposition forces every component, then every coefficient, to vanish. Conversely a nonzero zero decomposition supplies a nontrivial relation after expressing each component in its basis. Therefore

$$\dim(U_1+\cdots+U_s)=\sum_{i=1}^s\dim U_i \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\text{the sum is direct},$$

because a spanning list is a basis exactly when its length equals the dimension, by Corollary 19.6. Pairwise trivial intersections alone still do not suffice when $s\ge3$.

19.8 Count bases over a finite field

In $\mathbb F_2^3$, a first basis vector has $8-1$ choices. After choosing it, its span has two elements, leaving $8-2$ choices for the second. Their independent span has four distinct combinations, leaving $8-4$ choices for the third. Thus there are $7\cdot6\cdot4=168$ ordered bases. The program tests every ordered triple directly, without using a floating-point rank test.

from itertools import product

points = list(product(range(2), repeat=3))


def span(vectors):
    return {
        tuple(sum(a * v[j] for a, v in zip(coefficients, vectors)) % 2
              for j in range(3))
        for coefficients in product(range(2), repeat=len(vectors))
    }


counts = []
for length in range(4):
    independent = sum(
        len(span(vectors)) == 2 ** length
        for vectors in product(points, repeat=length)
    )
    counts.append(independent)
print(counts)
assert counts == [1, 7, 42, 168]
print(7 * 6 * 4)
[1, 7, 42, 168]
168

The empty list contributes one independent list of length zero. The span-size test is equivalent to independence here: the coefficient map has $2^r$ inputs, and its outputs are all distinct exactly when coefficients are unique. This exhaustive computation checks a finite instance of the theory; exchange proves the general theorem.

19.9 Problems with complete solutions

Problem 1. A parameter changes independence

Over $\mathbb R$, determine when $(1,0,1),(0,1,1),(1,1,t)$ is a basis of $\mathbb R^3$.

A zero relation gives $a+c=0$, $b+c=0$, and $a+b+tc=0$. Substitution leaves $(t-2)c=0$. If $t\ne2$, all coefficients vanish, and the length-three independent list is a basis. If $t=2$, the third vector is the sum of the first two, so it is dependent and not a basis. The first two remain independent, so their span then has dimension two.

Problem 2. A polynomial constraint

For $U=\{p\in P_3(F):p(a)=0\}$ with $a\in F$, find a basis and a complement.

Division by $X-a$ gives $p=(X-a)q+p(a)$. Thus every member of $U$ is uniquely $(X-a)q$ with $q\in P_2(F)$. A basis is $X-a,X(X-a),X^2(X-a)$: they span by expanding $q$, and a relation factors as $(X-a)(b_0+b_1X+b_2X^2)=0$, forcing all $b_i=0$ by the polynomial integral-domain property. Constants give a complement because every $p$ splits as $(p-p(a))+p(a)$, while a constant vanishing at $a$ is zero. Hence $\dim U=3$ over every field.

Problem 3. Two planes and their common line

In $F^3$, let $U=\{(x,y,0)\}$ and $W=\{(0,y,z)\}$. Compute the dimensions of their intersection and sum.

The intersection is $Fe_2$, of dimension one. The individual bases $(e_1,e_2)$ and $(e_2,e_3)$ show both planes have dimension two. Their sum contains all three standard basis vectors and hence is $F^3$, of dimension three. The formula gives $2+2-1=3$. The sum is not direct because its intersection contains the nonzero vector $e_2$.

Problem 4. Find a complement without an inner product

Let $U=\operatorname{span}(e_1+e_2,e_2+e_3)$ in $F^3$. Prove that $Fe_2$ is a complement and give the decomposition of $(x,y,z)$.

We have $(x,y,z)=x(e_1+e_2)+z(e_2+e_3)+(y-x-z)e_2$, so the proposed sum is all of $F^3$. A relation $a(e_1+e_2)+b(e_2+e_3)+ce_2=0$ gives $a=0$ from the first coordinate, $b=0$ from the third, then $c=0$ from the second. The combined list is a basis; the two blocks therefore form a direct sum. This proof works in every characteristic.

Problem 5. Why finite support matters

Prove that the coordinate sequences form a basis of the finite-support subspace of $F^{\mathbb N}$ in the sense of unique finite expressions, but do not span all sequences.

A finite-support sequence $x$ equals the finite sum $\sum_{j\in\operatorname{supp}x}x_je_j$. If a finite relation among the $e_j$ is zero, inspect each indicated coordinate to obtain its coefficient zero. Thus finite representations are unique. The all-one sequence has infinitely many nonzero coordinates because $1\ne0$; no finite combination of coordinate sequences can equal it. This identifies precisely the smaller space represented by that infinite family.

Problem 6. Dimension is finer than finite versus uncountable

Show that a dimension-$n$ space over a field of $q$ elements has $q^n$ elements. Explain why knowing only that a real vector space is uncountable does not determine its dimension.

A basis gives a bijection $F^n\to V$ by sending coefficients to their linear combination: spanning gives surjectivity and independence gives injectivity. There are exactly $q$ choices for each of $n$ coordinates, so finite counting gives $q^n$; the zero space has $q^0=1$ element. Both $\mathbb R$ and $\mathbb R^2$ are uncountable, the latter because it contains $\{(x,0):x\in\mathbb R\}$, while their dimensions are one and two. Thus the coarse label “uncountable” does not determine dimension. This argument does not prove that the two sets have equal cardinality; that stronger statement is a separate set-theoretic result.

19.10 References and boundaries

Lessons 34–35 are primary. Axler's Linear Algebra Done Right, fourth edition, §§2A–2C, printed pp.28–29,31–36,39–42,44–48, supports the dependence lemma, exchange, basis extraction and extension, complements, and dimension formula. Axler works over the real or complex numbers; the proofs here explicitly use only field axioms unless a different field is stated. Infinite-dimensional examples do not assert a construction of a basis for every vector space. The finite-field enumeration and all worked problems are teaching developments.
